% ABSTRACT
% =============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Quantum Cognition Machine Learning (QCML) encodes data as quantum
states in a Hilbert space and represents features as Hermitian
operators, inducing a rich geometric and topological structure over
the data manifold.  While QCML has been applied to intrinsic dimension
estimation, financial forecasting, and supervised similarity learning,
extracting geometric observables for time-series regime detection
remains unexplored.  We construct an error Hamiltonian from financial
features, compute the quasi-coherent ground states, and extract three
scalar observables from the induced quantum geometry: the Berry phase
rate, the quantum Fisher information (QFI) determinant, and a
multi-lag fidelity measure.  In a comparison of 17~methods across
12~historical crises, these QCML observables---fully
unsupervised---achieve effect sizes competitive with a supervised
Random Forest baseline ($d \approx 0.93$ vs.\ $d = 1.13$) and
substantially outperform standard unsupervised methods (BOCPD
$d = 0.59$, Isolation Forest $d = 0.46$).  Our central finding is
\emph{complementarity}: QCML observables excel on structurally novel
crises where supervised methods lack training precedent, and a hybrid
ensemble nearly doubles out-of-sample performance ($d = 0.60$ vs.\
$d = 0.31$).  Across five equity ETFs, Multi-Lag Fidelity achieves
$d = 1.44$ vs.\ RF $d = 0.88$ (Friedman $p < 0.0001$), demonstrating
cross-asset generalization.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} QCML, quantum cognition, regime detection,
Berry curvature, quantum metric, Fisher information, financial crises,
geometric observables.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% --- 1.1 Background and Motivation ---
\subsection{Background and Motivation}\label{subsec:background}

Quantum Cognition Machine Learning (QCML) is a framework in which data
points are represented as quantum states---unit vectors in a
finite-dimensional Hilbert space---and features or variables are
represented by Hermitian operators acting on those
states~\cite{musaelian2024,candelori2025}.  The key innovation is that
observables are operators rather than numbers: the expected value of a
Hermitian operator $A$ on a state $\ket{\psi}$ is
$\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}$, a number with uncertainty around it, and the
non-commutativity of operators encodes context-dependent relationships
among features~\cite{musaelian2024}.  This ``quantum encoding'' enables
QCML to learn broad abstractions from small amounts of data, achieving
a logarithmic economy of representation that overcomes the curse of
dimensionality inherent in classical statistical
approaches~\cite{musaelian2024,candelori2025}.

Detecting regime transitions---shifts between qualitatively different
market states---is a central problem in quantitative finance.  Markets
alternate between low-volatility trends, high-volatility drawdowns,
and liquidity crises, and the ability to identify transitions in real
time has implications for risk management, portfolio construction, and
hedging.  Classical approaches include Hidden Markov Models
(HMMs)~\cite{hamilton1989}, Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection
(BOCPD)~\cite{adams_mackay2007}, and supervised classifiers such as
Random Forests.  While effective in specific settings, these methods
operate in flat Euclidean feature spaces and struggle with structurally
novel crises that differ from training data.

Our central idea is that QCML induces quantum geometry over the data
manifold, and that this geometry changes when market regimes shift.
Specifically, the error Hamiltonian of QCML~\cite{candelori2025}
defines a smooth map from feature space to projective Hilbert space
$\CP^{n-1}$, endowing the data with a Fubini--Study metric, Berry
curvature, spectral gap, and quantum Fisher information.  These
geometric objects provide a family of scalar observables that respond
to structural changes in the market state space without requiring
labeled crisis data.

% --- 1.2 Prior Work ---
\subsection{Prior Work}\label{subsec:prior_work}

QCML was introduced by Musaelian et al.~\cite{musaelian2024} as a
practical machine learning framework inspired by quantum cognition, the
hypothesis that human decision-making follows quantum probability
rather than classical probability~\cite{busemeyer2012}.  Candelori et
al.~\cite{candelori2025} demonstrated that QCML encodes data as
quantum geometry, showing that the quantum metric tensor captures the
intrinsic dimension of datasets robustly even under noise.  Abanov et
al.~\cite{abanov2025} developed the mathematical theory of this
quantum geometry, including Berry curvature, Chern numbers, and the
matrix Laplacian, providing the formal tools we employ.

In the financial domain, Samson et al.~\cite{samson2024} applied QCML
to financial forecasting, demonstrating its ability to accommodate
large numbers of features with logarithmic scaling.  Rosaler et
al.~\cite{rosaler2025} applied QCML to supervised similarity learning
for corporate bonds, showing advantages over tree-based methods in
high-yield markets.  Samson et al.~\cite{samson2025} extended QCML
similarity to firm linkages, constructing profitable momentum spillover
strategies.  Di~Caro et al.~\cite{dicaro2025} applied QCML to
biomedical prediction, demonstrating superior performance in
high-dimensional, low-sample-size settings.

Classical regime detection originates with Hamilton's Markov-switching
autoregression~\cite{hamilton1989}, and Adams and
MacKay~\cite{adams_mackay2007} introduced BOCPD for online changepoint
detection.  Supervised classifiers achieve high in-sample accuracy but
require labeled data and are prone to overfitting on rare events.

The gap we address is that no prior work has extracted geometric
observables from QCML-induced quantum geometry for time-series regime
detection.

% --- 1.3 Problem Statement ---
\subsection{Problem Statement and Goals}\label{subsec:problem}

\paragraph{Research question.}  Can geometric observables derived from
QCML-induced quantum geometry detect financial regime transitions
without labeled data?

\paragraph{Objectives.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{O1.}] Construct a Riemannian geometry over financial time
  series via the QCML error Hamiltonian framework.
\item[\textbf{O2.}] Derive scalar observables (Berry curvature rate,
  QFI determinant, multi-lag fidelity) from this geometry.
\item[\textbf{O3.}] Validate against classical and supervised baselines
  on 12 historical crises across multiple assets.
\end{enumerate}

% --- 1.4 Contributions ---
\subsection{Contributions}\label{subsec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{First application of QCML geometric observables to
  financial regime detection.}  We extract Berry curvature, quantum
  metric, and spectral gap from QCML-induced geometry and show they
  carry rich information about market regime structure.

\item \textbf{Three novel observables.}  Berry Phase Rate, QFI
  Determinant, and Multi-Lag Fidelity probe distinct geometric aspects
  of regime transitions (curvature change, manifold volume, state
  stability).

\item \textbf{Empirical evidence of complementarity.}  QCML observables
  and supervised methods detect structurally different crisis types; a
  hybrid ensemble nearly doubles out-of-sample performance.

\item \textbf{Cross-asset generalization.}  QCML observables
  generalize across five equity ETFs, with Multi-Lag Fidelity
  significantly outperforming Random Forest ($d = 1.44$ vs.\
  $d = 0.88$, Friedman $p < 0.0001$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Framework}\label{sec:theory}

% --- 2.1 Quantum Foundations in QCML ---
\subsection{Quantum Foundations in QCML}\label{subsec:qcml_foundations}

We work in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
$\calH \cong \C^n$~\cite{candelori2025,abanov2025}.  A \emph{state}
is a unit vector $\ket{\psi}\in\calH$ with $\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1$,
and an \emph{observable} is a Hermitian operator $A = A^\dagger$ acting
on $\calH$.  The expectation value
$\langle A \rangle = \bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}$ is a real number, but
unlike classical statistics, the outcome carries intrinsic uncertainty
governed by the operator's spectrum.

Given a data point $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in\R^d$ and a set of learned
Hermitian operators $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^d$, the QCML \emph{error
Hamiltonian}~\cite{candelori2025,musaelian2024} is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
  H(x) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d} (A_k - x_k\, I)^2\,,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the identity.  The Hamiltonian $H(x)$ is Hermitian and
positive semi-definite, measuring the collective ``error'' between the
operators $\{A_k\}$ and the target values $\{x_k\}$.

\begin{definition}[Quasi-coherent state~\cite{candelori2025}]
\label{def:qcs}
The \emph{quasi-coherent state} at $x$ is the ground state of $H(x)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ground_state}
  \ket{\psi(x)} = \argmin_{\ket{\phi},\,\|\phi\|=1}
  \dbraket{\phi}{H(x)}{\phi}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The map $x\mapsto\ket{\psi(x)}$ is a smooth embedding
$\R^d\hookrightarrow\CP^{n-1}$ wherever the spectral gap
$\Delta(x) = E_1(x) - E_0(x) > 0$~\cite{candelori2025,abanov2025}.
This QCML embedding lifts each data point into projective Hilbert
space, endowing the data manifold with quantum geometry.

The pullback of the Fubini--Study metric~\cite{provost_vallee1980} to
$\R^d$ defines the \emph{quantum metric tensor} (the quantum analogue
of the classical Fisher information metric~\cite{amari_nagaoka2000}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric}
  g_{ab}(x) = \mathrm{Re}\,\braket{\partial_a\psi}{\partial_b\psi}
  - \braket{\partial_a\psi}{\psi}\braket{\psi}{\partial_b\psi}\,,
\end{equation}
and the \emph{Berry curvature}~\cite{berry1984,abanov2025}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:berry}
  F_{ab}(x) = -2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\braket{\partial_a\psi}{\partial_b\psi}\,.
\end{equation}
Together, $g_{ab}$ and $F_{ab}$ form the real and imaginary parts of
the quantum geometric tensor, the central mathematical object of
QCML geometry~\cite{abanov2025}.  Candelori et
al.~\cite{candelori2025} showed that the rank of $g_{ab}$ at a point
$x$ equals the local intrinsic dimension of the data manifold, and
that the spectral gap of $g_{ab}$ robustly estimates global intrinsic
dimension even under noise.

% --- 2.2 Quantum Cognition and QCML ---
\subsection{Quantum Cognition and QCML}\label{subsec:qcml_cognition}

The intellectual foundation of QCML lies in quantum cognition: the
hypothesis that states of knowledge are naturally represented as
vectors in a Hilbert space, with questions and observables as
operators~\cite{busemeyer2012,musaelian2024}.  Unlike classical
probability, where concepts are represented by sets and the complexity
of their description grows exponentially with the number of features,
quantum representations achieve a logarithmic economy---the number of
parameters in a QCML model scales quadratically with the number of
features, not exponentially~\cite{musaelian2024}.

This has practical consequences.  Classical machine learning requires
exponentially more data as features increase (the curse of
dimensionality).  QCML, by encoding features as non-commuting
operators rather than as coordinates in a probability simplex, captures
interactions between features in the off-diagonal structure of the
Hermitian operators without explicitly enumerating all
combinations~\cite{musaelian2024,candelori2025}.  The ``uncertainty''
inherent in quantum representations is not a bug but a feature: it
enables abstraction, generalization, and the formation of concepts
from limited data~\cite{musaelian2024}.

Training a QCML model involves learning the operators $\{A_k\}$ by
iteratively computing the ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ of the error
Hamiltonian and updating $A_k$ via gradient descent to reduce the
ground state energy~\cite{musaelian2024,samson2024}.

% --- 2.3 QCML Perspective on Regime Detection ---
\subsection{QCML Perspective on Regime Detection}%
\label{subsec:qcml_regime}

In our application, financial features are embedded as Hermitian
operators in a Hilbert space via the error Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:hamiltonian}.
The resulting quasi-coherent states $\ket{\psi(x_t)}$ trace a path
through $\CP^{n-1}$ as market conditions evolve.  We interpret regime
detection through the lens of QCML geometry:

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{regime} is a region of feature space where the quantum
  geometric properties (metric, curvature, spectral gap) are
  approximately stationary.

\item A \textbf{regime transition} is a geometric phase change:
  Berry curvature spikes, the spectral gap collapses, or state
  fidelity drops rapidly---analogous to quantum phase transitions in
  condensed matter~\cite{sachdev2011}.
\end{itemize}

The following formal results establish the mathematical foundations.

\begin{theorem}[Smoothness of the QCML embedding]\label{thm:smoothness}
Let $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^d$ be Hermitian operators and let $H(x)$ be the
error Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:hamiltonian}.  If the spectral gap
$\Delta(x) > 0$ for all $x$ in an open set $U\subset\R^d$, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The ground state map
  $x \mapsto \ket{\psi(x)} \in \CP^{n-1}$ is $C^\infty$ on $U$.
\item The quantum metric tensor $g_{ab}(x)$ and Berry curvature
  $F_{ab}(x)$ are $C^\infty$ on $U$.
\item All geometric observables derived from $g_{ab}$ and $F_{ab}$
  are $C^\infty$ on $U$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\noindent\emph{Proof.}  By the implicit function theorem applied to
the eigenvalue equation; see Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs}.

\begin{theorem}[Chern number quantization~\cite{abanov2025}]%
\label{thm:chern}
Let $S\subset\R^d$ be a closed, oriented 2-surface over which
$\Delta > 0$.  Then the first Chern number
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chern_thm}
  C_1 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_S F_{ab}\, dx^a \wedge dx^b
  \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
is an integer, invariant under smooth deformations preserving
$\Delta > 0$.
\end{theorem}
\noindent\emph{Proof.}  Chern--Weil theorem applied to the $U(1)$
line bundle; see Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs}.

\paragraph{Important caveat.}
In our empirical application, we compute curvature integrals over
\emph{rolling windows}---open subsets of parameter space---yielding
non-integer values.  The topological protection argument (noise cannot
change an integer) does not strictly apply.  Nevertheless, the
magnitude of these curvature integrals is empirically informative, and
sharp changes between windows are associated with regime boundaries.

\begin{theorem}[Curvature--gap bound]\label{thm:curvature_gap}
For any pair of indices $(a,b)$ with $a\neq b$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature_gap_bound}
  |F_{ab}(x)| \;\leq\; \frac{K}{\Delta(x)^2}\,,
\end{equation}
where $K$ depends on the Hamiltonian derivatives but not on the gap.
\end{theorem}
\noindent This bound formalizes the detection mechanism: as the
spectral gap $\Delta\to 0$ at a regime boundary, Berry curvature can
diverge as $\Delta^{-2}$, producing the spikes our detectors
identify.  See Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs} for the proof.

\begin{proposition}[QFI volume interpretation]\label{prop:qfi_volume}
The pseudo-determinant $\det^+(g) = \prod_{\lambda_i > \epsilon}
\lambda_i$ equals the squared Riemannian volume element of the
non-degenerate subspace of the quantum metric.  Thus:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\det^+(g) \to 0$ indicates dimensional collapse
  (approaching a phase boundary).
\item Large $\det^+(g)$ indicates the state is exploring a
  high-dimensional region of state space (stable regime).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof.}  Direct from the relationship between the
metric determinant and Riemannian volume; see
Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs}.

\section{Model and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

% --- 3.1 Model Description ---
\subsection{Model Description}\label{subsec:model}

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Daily OHLCV data for equity ETFs are transformed into five normalized
features (log returns, 20-day rolling volatility, z-scored volume,
relative strength, momentum), reduced to $p = 15$ principal components,
and $L^2$-normalized.

\paragraph{QCML embedding.}
The $p$-dimensional feature vector $x_t$ at each time step is fed to
the error Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:hamiltonian} with $n \times n$
Hermitian operators $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^p$ ($n = 8$ by default).  The
ground state $\ket{\psi(x_t)}$ is computed via eigendecomposition
of $H(x_t)$.

\paragraph{Operator construction.}
We use \emph{PCA-inspired} operators: Pauli-basis matrices (tensor
products of Pauli matrices for $n = 2^q$) scaled by the PCA
eigenvalues, $A_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}\,\sigma_k$.  This approximates
the data covariance structure in the QCML operator
space~\cite{candelori2025}.  Full gradient-descent training of
operators~\cite{musaelian2024,samson2024} is left for future work.

\paragraph{Outputs.}
Three geometric observables are computed from the quantum geometry at
each time step and converted to regime signals via adaptive
thresholding.

% --- 3.2 Observable Extraction ---
\subsection{Observable Extraction}\label{subsec:observables}

The QCML embedding induces a family of scalar observables that probe
distinct aspects of the quantum geometry.

\subsubsection{Berry Phase Rate}
The rate of Berry curvature change between consecutive time steps:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:berry_rate}
  \dot{\gamma}(t) = |F_{01}(x_t) - F_{01}(x_{t-1})|\,,
\end{equation}
where $F_{01}$ is the Berry curvature in the first two PCA directions.
A spike in $\dot{\gamma}$ indicates the QCML state is crossing a
geometric phase boundary.

\subsubsection{QFI Determinant}
The pseudo-determinant of the quantum metric tensor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfi_det}
  \det^+(g(x_t)) = \prod_{\lambda_i > \epsilon} \lambda_i\,,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon = 10^{-10}$.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:qfi_volume},
this measures the Riemannian volume element of the QCML data
manifold.  Collapse signals approaching a phase boundary; expansion
signals rapid state-space exploration.

\subsubsection{Multi-Lag Fidelity}
Weighted average infidelity across multiple time lags:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multilag}
  \bar{\mathcal{I}}(t) = \sum_j w_j\,
  \bigl(1-|\braket{\psi(x_t)}{\psi(x_{t-\delta_j})}|^2\bigr)\,,
\end{equation}
with $\delta \in \{1,3,5,10\}$ days and weights
$[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]$.  Short lags detect fast crises; long lags
detect gradual regime drift.  The multi-lag structure captures how
rapidly the QCML state decorrelates from its recent history.

\subsubsection{Spectral Gap}
The energy difference $\Delta(t) = E_1(t) - E_0(t)$ between the
two lowest eigenvalues.  By analogy with quantum phase
transitions~\cite{sachdev2011}, a closing gap signals the approach to
a critical point where the ground state becomes ill-defined.

\paragraph{Methods.}
We compare 17 methods: 7 classical/supervised baselines (Rolling Vol~Z,
CUSUM, HMM, BOCPD~\cite{adams_mackay2007}, Isolation Forest, Random

$\pm 10$-day windows around the crisis date.

Each method--crisis pair is evaluated via Welch's $t$-test

($n = 5{,}000$), and Bayes factor.

% --- 3.4 Implementation Details ---
\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{subsec:implementation}

The pipeline is implemented in Python using NumPy and SciPy.  Market
data are obtained via the Polygon.io API.  The error
Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:hamiltonian} is constructed for each time step,
and eigendecomposition is performed via \texttt{numpy.linalg.eigh}.
Partial derivatives for the quantum metric and Berry curvature are
computed via central finite differences with $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$.
Hyperparameter optimization uses Optuna TPE with leave-one-crisis-out
cross-validation.

Default hyperparameters: Hilbert dimension $n = 8$, PCA components
$p = 15$, PCA-inspired operators, rolling window $w = 20$ days,
fidelity lags $\{1, 3, 5, 10\}$ with weights
$[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:detection} summarizes the detection pipeline.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{QCML Geometric Regime Detection Pipeline}\label{alg:detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Feature matrix $X\in\R^{T\times d}$, Hilbert dimension $n$,
  operators $\{A_k\}$
\Ensure Geometric observable time series, regime signals
\State \textbf{Preprocessing:} Standardize $X$; PCA to $p$
  components; $L^2$-normalize rows
\State \textbf{Operator construction:} Fit $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^p$ via
  PCA-inspired method
\For{each time point $t=1,\ldots,T$}
  \State Compute $H(x_t)$ via Equation~\ref{eq:hamiltonian}
  \State Solve $\ket{\psi(x_t)}=$ ground state of $H(x_t)$
  \State Record $E_0(t)$, $\Delta(t)$,
    $\mathcal{F}(t,t-\delta)$ for each lag $\delta$
\EndFor
\State Compute Berry phase rate $\dot{\gamma}(t)$
  via Equation~\ref{eq:berry_rate}
\State Compute QFI pseudo-determinant $\det^+(g(x_t))$
  via Equation~\ref{eq:qfi_det}
\State Compute multi-lag infidelity $\bar{\mathcal{I}}(t)$
  via Equation~\ref{eq:multilag}
\State Apply adaptive thresholds to generate regime signals
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Quantitative Performance}\label{subsec:main_results}

\paragraph{Granger causality.}
We test Granger causality~\cite{granger1969} in both directions
using 3 observables $\times$ 3 market targets $\times$ 5 lags
(90 tests, Bonferroni corrected).  QFI Determinant Granger-causes
absolute returns at lag~1 ($F = 9.5$, $p = 0.0004$,
Bonferroni-significant).  However, reverse causality (market
$\to$ QCML) is substantially stronger (17/45 nominally significant
vs.\ 6/45 forward), indicating that most observables react to
rather than predict market stress---consistent with their
construction from contemporaneous data.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter sensitivity.}
Berry Phase Rate and QFI Determinant peak at $n = 8$; both degrade at
$n = 16$.  Multi-Lag Fidelity is the most robust (CV~24.6\%).
PCA-inspired operators consistently outperform random initialization
(+0.06 to +0.23 mean~$d$).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

% --- 5.1 Interpretation ---
\subsection{Interpretation}\label{subsec:interpretation}

The most striking finding is the systematic interaction between method
type and crisis type.  We classify crises into two categories:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conventional crises}: events resembling prior episodes
  in the training set (2007, 2008, 2010, 2011, 2015, 2016, 2020).
  RF leverages pattern similarity with labeled data for strong
  detection.

\item \textbf{Novel crises}: structurally unfamiliar events involving
  new market mechanisms (2018 Volmageddon, 2018 Fed Selloff, 2019
  Repo, 2022 Rate Hikes, 2023 SVB).  QCML methods detect geometric
  deformations of the state space regardless of whether such changes
  have been observed before.
\end{itemize}

This complementarity has a natural interpretation within the QCML
framework.  Random Forest detects crises by pattern matching in flat
feature space.  QCML observables detect crises by measuring curvature,
topology, and metric deformation of the quantum data
manifold~\cite{abanov2025}---a fundamentally different signal that
responds to structural change in the curved QCML state space.

Three lines of evidence support the complementarity thesis:
\textbf{(i)}~bimodal performance (QCML dominates on novel crises while
RF dominates on conventional ones); \textbf{(ii)}~temporal robustness
(QCML shows minimal OOS degradation while RF degrades on novel
crises); \textbf{(iii)}~ensemble improvement (hybrid doubles OOS
performance, confirming non-redundant information content).

% --- 5.2 Relation to QCML Literature ---
\subsection{Relation to QCML}\label{subsec:relation_qcml}

Our work validates and extends QCML in several directions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Geometry carries meaningful signal.}  Candelori et
  al.~\cite{candelori2025} showed that the quantum metric captures
  intrinsic dimension; we show that the \emph{dynamics} of this
  geometry---how it changes over time---carries rich information about
  regime structure.

\item \textbf{New application domain.}  Prior QCML finance work
  focused on forecasting~\cite{samson2024} and
  similarity~\cite{rosaler2025,samson2025}; we extend to
  anomaly/regime detection, demonstrating the versatility of QCML
  geometric observables.

\item \textbf{Berry curvature as a practical tool.}  Abanov et
  al.~\cite{abanov2025} developed the theory of Berry curvature and
  Chern numbers in QCML; we provide the first empirical application,
  showing that Berry curvature spikes correspond to genuine market
  regime transitions.

\item \textbf{Analogy with quantum phase transitions.}  The spectral
  gap closure near regime boundaries mirrors the phenomenology of
  quantum phase transitions~\cite{sachdev2011}.  While the analogy is
  formal (our system is not quantum), the mathematical structure
  provides powerful detection tools via
  Theorem~\ref{thm:curvature_gap}.
\end{enumerate}

% --- 5.3 Limitations ---
\subsection{Limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Heuristic operators.}  We use PCA-inspired operator
  construction rather than full QCML gradient-descent
  training~\cite{musaelian2024,samson2024}.  While this approximation
  is effective (PCA-inspired outperforms random by +0.23 mean~$d$),
  learned operators should further improve performance.

\item \textbf{No individual method beats RF.}  After Holm--Bonferroni
  correction, none of the QCML observables individually achieves a
  statistically significant advantage over the supervised RF baseline.

\item \textbf{Non-integer Chern numbers.}  Rolling-window curvature
  integrals are non-integer, invalidating strict topological
  protection arguments~\cite{abanov2025}.

\item \textbf{Fusion overfitting.}  Optimized fusion achieves
  $d = 1.67$ on the calibration set but $d = 1.09$ in nested LOCO
  validation---a 35.6\% gap.

\item \textbf{Asset class coverage.}  All validation uses equity ETFs;
  whether the geometric signal persists in fixed income~\cite{rosaler2025},
  commodities, or FX remains untested.

\item \textbf{Reverse Granger causality.}  Market $\to$ QCML
  causality is stronger than QCML $\to$ market, indicating the
  observables largely react to rather than predict stress.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Full QCML operator training.}  Replace PCA-inspired
  operators with gradient-descent-trained operators, optimizing the
  error Hamiltonian on financial data as
  in~\cite{musaelian2024,samson2024}.  This should improve geometric
  observable quality.

\item \textbf{Supervised QCML regime detection.}  Train operators to
  maximize regime detection performance directly, combining the QCML
  geometry with labeled crisis data.

\item \textbf{Cross-asset QCML geometry.}  Apply QCML geometric
  observables to corporate bonds~\cite{rosaler2025} and equity
  firm linkages~\cite{samson2025} for regime detection in those
  markets.

\item \textbf{QCML POVM for probabilistic forecasts.}  Di~Caro et
  al.~\cite{dicaro2025} introduced QCML POVM for probabilistic
  prediction; adapting this for regime probability estimation would
  extend our binary detection framework.

\item \textbf{Real-time streaming deployment.}  Efficient incremental
  computation of QCML observables for streaming financial data.
\end{enumerate}

% 7. CONCLUSION

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that the quantum geometry induced by QCML's error
Hamiltonian provides a principled framework for unsupervised financial
regime detection.  Three observables---Berry Phase Rate, QFI
Determinant, and Multi-Lag Fidelity---derived from the quantum metric
tensor and Berry curvature of the QCML data manifold, achieve large
effect sizes competitive with supervised methods and substantially
outperform standard unsupervised baselines.

Our central finding is complementarity: QCML observables and
supervised classifiers detect structurally different crisis types.  On
novel crises where supervised methods lack training precedent, QCML
observables maintain detection power, and a hybrid ensemble nearly
doubles out-of-sample performance.  Across five equity ETFs, Multi-Lag
Fidelity significantly outperforms Random Forest, demonstrating that
QCML geometry captures universal features of regime transitions.

These results position QCML geometric observables as a complement to
existing regime detection methods, with greatest value precisely where
pattern-matching approaches fail.  Full QCML operator training via
gradient descent---replacing the PCA-inspired approximation used
here---should further improve performance and represents the natural
next step toward a complete QCML-based regime detection system.

\section{Proofs of Formal Results}\label{sec:proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}]
Consider the eigenvalue equation
$H(x)\ket{\psi(x)} = E_0(x)\ket{\psi(x)}$ subject to
$\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1$.  Define
$\Phi(x,\psi,E) = (H(x)\psi - E\psi,\; \|\psi\|^2-1)$.  We have
$\Phi(x_0,\psi_0,E_0)=0$.  The Jacobian with respect to $(\psi,E)$ is
\[
  D_{(\psi,E)}\Phi = \begin{pmatrix}
    H(x_0)-E_0\,I & -\psi_0 \\
    2\psi_0^\dagger & 0
  \end{pmatrix}\,.
\]
The operator $H(x_0)-E_0\,I$ restricted to $\psi_0^\perp$ is
invertible with smallest singular value $\Delta(x_0)>0$.  By the
implicit function theorem~\cite{kato1966}, $x\mapsto(\psi(x), E_0(x))$
is $C^\infty$ wherever $\Delta>0$.  Since $H(x)$ is polynomial in $x$,
$\Phi$ is $C^\infty$ in all arguments.  Smoothness of $g_{ab}$ and
$F_{ab}$ follows from their definitions as derivatives of
$\ket{\psi(x)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:chern}]
The ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ defines a $U(1)$ line bundle
$\mathcal{L}$ over $S$.  The Berry connection
$\mathcal{A}_a = i\braket{\psi}{\partial_a\psi}$ has curvature
$F_{ab}$.  By the Chern--Weil theorem~\cite{chern1946,nakahara2003}:
\[
  \oint_S \frac{F}{2\pi} = c_1(\mathcal{L}) \in \mathbb{Z}\,.
\]
The invariance under smooth deformations follows from
$c_1 \in H^2(S;\mathbb{Z})$ being a cohomological invariant.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:curvature_gap}]
First-order perturbation theory gives:
\[
  F_{ab} = -2\,\mathrm{Im}\sum_{n\neq 0}
  \frac{\dbraket{\psi_0}{\partial_a H}{\psi_n}
        \dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_b H}{\psi_0}}{(E_n-E_0)^2}\,.
\]
Since $E_n - E_0 \geq \Delta$ for all $n \neq 0$:
\[
  |F_{ab}| \leq \frac{1}{\Delta^2}\;
  2\sum_{n\neq 0}
  |\dbraket{\psi_0}{\partial_a H}{\psi_n}|\,
  |\dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_b H}{\psi_0}|
  = \frac{K}{\Delta^2}\,,
\]
where $K$ depends on matrix elements of $\partial_a H$ between
eigenstates but not on the gap.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:qfi_volume}]
Let $g_{ab}$ have eigenvalues
$\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_r > 0 = \lambda_{r+1}=\cdots$.
The Riemannian volume element on the non-degenerate $r$-dimensional
subspace is $\mathrm{vol}_r = \sqrt{\det(g|_r)}$, and
$\det(g|_r) = \prod_{i=1}^r \lambda_i = \det^+(g)$.  Thus
$\det^+(g) = (\mathrm{vol}_r)^2$.
\end{proof}
