% ABSTRACT
% =============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Classical regime detection methods operate in flat Euclidean feature
spaces, relying on distributional assumptions that are violated during
the very transitions they seek to detect.  We construct a learned
Riemannian manifold for financial markets by lifting time series into a
projective Hilbert space $\CP^{n-1}$ via an error Hamiltonian and
extracting the induced geometric structure: quantum metric tensor,
Berry curvature, spectral gap, and quantum Fisher information (QFI)
matrix.  This geometry induces a family of scalar
observables---Berry phase rate, QFI pseudo-determinant, rolling Chern
numbers, multi-lag fidelity, and metric condition number---that
summarize the local and global structure of the market manifold at each
point in time.  In a head-to-head comparison of 16 methods across 12
historical crises (2007--2023), we show that:
\textbf{(i)}~Optuna-optimized fusion of the three top geometric
observables achieves median Cohen's $d = 1.67$ across all
crises---48\% above the supervised Random Forest baseline ($d = 1.13$)
despite requiring no labeled training data;
\textbf{(ii)}~under a strictly causal protocol that prevents lookahead
bias, individual geometric observables achieve $d > 2.0$ (QFI
$d = 2.30$, multi-lag fidelity $d = 2.29$);
\textbf{(iii)}~the geometric framework generalizes across asset classes:
all three methods maintain $d > 1.0$ on five broad-market ETFs
(SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA, DIA) with Friedman $\chi^2 = 35.0$, $p < 0.0001$;
and \textbf{(iv)}~the geometric signal is complementary to supervised
detection---on novel crises (SVB, COVID), QCML achieves $d > 1.7$
where Random Forest scores $d < 0.5$, while a hybrid ensemble nearly
doubles out-of-sample $d$ relative to RF alone ($d = 0.60$ vs.\ $d = 0.31$).
These findings establish the learned market manifold as a principled
geometric framework whose topological and curvature properties encode
regime structure, with greatest value precisely where supervised
approaches are weakest.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} regime detection, quantum geometry,
Chern number, Berry curvature, quantum metric, topological invariants,
financial crises, quantum Fisher information.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Detecting regime transitions is a central problem in quantitative
finance.  Market dynamics alternate between qualitatively different
states---low-volatility trends, high-volatility drawdowns, liquidity
crises---and the ability to identify transitions in real time has
implications for risk management, portfolio construction, and hedging.

Classical approaches include Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)
\cite{hamilton1989}, cumulative-sum (CUSUM) statistics
\cite{page1954}, rolling volatility breakpoints, and supervised
classifiers such as Random Forests.  While effective in specific
settings, these methods share a common limitation: they operate entirely
within a Euclidean feature space and rely on distributional assumptions
(Gaussian mixtures, stationarity within regimes) that are frequently
violated during the very transitions they seek to detect.

Our central thesis is that financial regimes correspond to distinct
topological phases of a learned market manifold, and that regime
transitions manifest as geometric singularities---curvature spikes,
spectral gap closures, and changes in topological charge---of this
manifold.

In this paper we construct a learned Riemannian manifold for financial
markets and show that regime detection emerges as a natural consequence
of its geometry.  Inspired by the Quantum Cognition Machine Learning
(QCML) framework \cite{qcml_ref}, we \emph{lift} the feature space
$\R^d$ into a projective Hilbert space $\CP^{n-1}$ via a family of
Hermitian operators.  The ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ of an error
Hamiltonian $H(x)$ defines a smooth map whose differential geometry
inherits topological structure from quantum mechanics.  Specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The \emph{quantum metric tensor} $g_{ab}$ (the real part of the
  Fubini--Study metric pulled back to $\R^d$) endows the data manifold
  with a natural Riemannian structure, replacing the ad hoc Euclidean
  metric.
\item The \emph{Berry curvature} $F_{ab}$ (the antisymmetric,
  imaginary part) \cite{wilczek_shapere1989} gives rise to topological
  invariants---\emph{Chern numbers}---that are integers for closed
  manifolds and therefore intrinsically robust to continuous perturbations
  (noise).
\item Discontinuities in the rolling Chern number distinguish
  \emph{topological} regime transitions ($\Delta C\neq 0$) from extreme
  events within the same regime ($\Delta C=0$), a distinction
  inaccessible to purely statistical detectors.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}  Our contributions form a three-layer
construction with empirical validation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The geometry.}  We construct the full
  Riemannian and topological geometry of a financial data manifold via
  the QCML error Hamiltonian, including the quantum metric tensor,
  Berry curvature, Christoffel symbols, Ricci scalar, spectral gap, and
  quantum distances (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}).
\item \textbf{Geometric dynamics.}  We formulate a stochastic differential
  equation whose diffusion tensor is derived from the inverse quantum
  metric, producing dynamics that respect the learned geometry
  (Section~\ref{sec:sde}).
\item \textbf{Geometric observables and their regime-detection
  consequences.}  The geometry induces a family of scalar
  observables---Berry phase rate, QFI pseudo-determinant, Chern numbers,
  multi-lag fidelity, metric condition number, and five additional
  indicators (Section~\ref{sec:observables}).  We show that these
  observables change sharply around known regime transitions across 12
  historical crises on five broad-market ETFs.  Optimized fusion of
  the top three observables achieves median $d = 1.67$, exceeding the
  supervised Random Forest baseline ($d = 1.13$) by 48\%, and individual
  methods reach $d > 2.0$ under strictly causal protocols
  (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Classical Regime Detection}\label{subsec:classical_rd}

The regime-switching framework originates with Hamilton's Markov-switching
autoregression \cite{hamilton1989}, in which a latent discrete state
governs the parameters of an observed process.  The two-state Gaussian
HMM remains a workhorse, estimating $P(\text{high-vol state}\,|\,r_t)$
via the Baum--Welch algorithm.  CUSUM statistics \cite{page1954} provide
a nonparametric alternative, accumulating deviations
$S_t = \max(0,\, S_{t-1}+|r_t|-k)$ and flagging $S_t>h$ as a
changepoint.  Rolling volatility z-scores are perhaps the simplest
approach: one computes
$z_t = (\sigma^{\text{short}}_t - \mu^{\text{long}}_t)/\sigma^{\text{long}}_t$
and applies a threshold.  Supervised classifiers (Random Forest,
gradient-boosted trees) can achieve high in-sample accuracy but require
labeled data and are prone to overfitting on rare events
\cite{gu2020_ml_finance}.

\paragraph{Bayesian online changepoint detection.}
Adams and MacKay \cite{adams_mackay2007} introduced Bayesian Online
Changepoint Detection (BOCPD), which maintains a posterior distribution
over the \emph{run length}---the time since the last changepoint---and
updates it recursively as new observations arrive.  Extensions to
multivariate settings and non-exponential-family likelihoods have been
explored by Knoblauch and Damoulas \cite{knoblauch2018}.  While BOCPD
provides principled uncertainty quantification over changepoint timing,
it remains a distributional method operating in flat Euclidean space.

All of these methods treat features as elements of a flat Euclidean
space.  Our approach differs by constructing a learned Riemannian
manifold whose curvature and topology encode structural information
about the market state---and whose geometric observables, as we show,
change sharply at regime boundaries.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired Machine Learning}\label{subsec:qiml}

The QCML framework \cite{qcml_ref} embeds classical data into quantum
mechanical structures.  Each data point $x\in\R^d$ is associated with a
Hamiltonian $H(x)$ whose ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ serves as a
nonlinear feature map $\R^d \to \CP^{n-1}$.  This is conceptually
analogous to kernel methods---the Fubini--Study metric induces a
positive-definite kernel
$K(x,y) = |\braket{\psi(x)}{\psi(y)}|^2$---but additionally provides
geometric and topological structure absent from standard kernel machines.

\subsection{Topological Data Analysis in Finance}\label{subsec:tda}

Topological data analysis (TDA) applies algebraic topology to point-cloud
data.  Gidea and Katz \cite{gidea_katz2018} used persistent homology on
sliding windows of financial time series, computing Betti numbers
$\beta_0$ (connected components) and $\beta_1$ (loops) of
Vietoris--Rips complexes to detect early warning signals of market
crashes.  Gidea \cite{gidea2017} extended this to cross-sectional
equity data, showing that changes in topological features precede major
drawdowns.  Persistent landscapes \cite{bubenik2015} and persistence
images \cite{adams2017} provide stable vectorizations of persistence
diagrams suitable for downstream machine learning.

Our approach differs from TDA in several respects.  First, TDA extracts
topology from point-cloud geometry (pairwise distances), while we extract
it from a \emph{learned} quantum-mechanical structure (Berry curvature
and Chern numbers).  Second, TDA typically computes global topological
invariants over a sliding window, whereas our Chern numbers are local
quantities defined at each point on the parameter manifold.  Third, our
framework provides a richer geometric structure (metric tensor, curvature,
spectral gap) beyond topology alone.  We compare directly to a TDA
baseline in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches to Regime Detection}\label{subsec:dl_rd}

Deep learning methods have been applied to regime detection with mixed
results.  Recurrent architectures such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks \cite{hochreiter1997} can model temporal dependencies in
financial time series, while Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs)
\cite{bai2018} offer parallelizable alternatives with dilated causal
convolutions.  Autoencoder-based anomaly detection
\cite{sakurada2014} identifies regime transitions as reconstruction
errors.  Attention mechanisms \cite{vaswani2017} have been applied to
changepoint detection, learning which temporal features are most
informative for regime classification.

These deep learning approaches are fundamentally supervised: they require
labeled regime data for training and tend to overfit on the small number
of historical crises available.  Our geometric observables are
unsupervised---they detect regime transitions as intrinsic geometric
singularities without labeled training data---making them particularly
valuable for detecting structurally novel crises.

\subsection{Geometric Methods in Finance}\label{subsec:geom_finance}

Information geometry \cite{amari_nagaoka2000} studies the differential
geometry of statistical models.  The Fisher information metric on the
space of probability distributions has been applied to financial time
series analysis \cite{brody_hughston2001}, where geodesic distances
between estimated distributions measure regime dissimilarity.  Our
quantum metric $g_{ab}$ is the natural generalization of the Fisher
metric to the projective Hilbert space setting: when the parametric
family $\ket{\psi(x)}$ is interpreted as a statistical model (via
Born's rule), $g_{ab}$ reduces to $\frac{1}{4}$ times the quantum
Fisher information matrix \cite{zanardi2007}.

\paragraph{Rough paths and path signatures.}
The theory of rough paths \cite{lyons1998} provides a coordinate-free
description of irregular paths, and the \emph{path signature}---the
iterated integrals of a path---serves as a universal nonlinear feature
map.  Chevyrev and Kormilitzin \cite{chevyrev2016} applied signatures to
sequential data classification, and Lyons et al.\ \cite{lyons2020}
demonstrated their effectiveness for financial time series analysis.  The
path signature captures order-dependent information (lead-lag
relationships) that Euclidean features miss.  Our approach is
complementary: while signatures characterize the path in a feature space,
our geometry characterizes the \emph{manifold structure} induced by
projecting into Hilbert space.

\subsection{Quantum Finance}\label{subsec:quantum_finance}

Quantum-inspired approaches to finance extend beyond the QCML framework.
Baaquie \cite{baaquie2004} formulated option pricing using path integrals
over field configurations, recovering Black--Scholes in appropriate
limits.  Haven and Khrennikov \cite{haven_khrennikov2013} developed
quantum probability models for decision-making under Knightian
uncertainty, showing that the non-commutativity of quantum observables
naturally models order effects and conjunction fallacies in behavioral
finance.  Piotrowski and S\l{}adkowski \cite{piotrowski2002} formulated
market games using quantum strategies.

Our work differs from quantum finance proper in that we do not posit
quantum mechanics as a fundamental model of markets; rather, we use the
\emph{mathematical machinery} of quantum mechanics (Hilbert spaces,
Hermitian operators, Berry phases) as a structured nonlinear embedding
that provides geometric and topological tools unavailable in classical
feature spaces.

\subsection{SDEs in Quantitative Finance}\label{subsec:sde_finance}

Stochastic differential equations are the standard modeling framework in
quantitative finance, from Black--Scholes \cite{black_scholes1973} to
the Heston stochastic volatility model \cite{heston1993}.  The geometric
SDE we construct in Section~\ref{sec:sde} differs from these classical
models in that its diffusion tensor is not assumed \emph{a priori} but
is \emph{learned} from the data via the quantum metric.

\section{QCML-Induced Market Geometry}\label{sec:geometry}

The central mathematical object of this paper is a Riemannian manifold
learned from financial data.  We construct it by lifting the feature
space into a projective Hilbert space via an error Hamiltonian, and then
extracting the induced geometric structure---quantum metric, Berry
curvature, spectral gap, and higher invariants---as properties of the
market state space.  All constructions are implemented in the
\texttt{QCMLGeometry} class; we include code-level references for
reproducibility.

\subsection{Error Hamiltonian and Quasi-Coherent States}

Let $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^d$ be a family of $n\times n$ Hermitian operators
(observables) learned from data.  Given a data point $x\in\R^d$, we
define the \emph{error Hamiltonian}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
  H(x) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d} (A_k - x_k\, I)^2\,,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix.  The Hamiltonian
$H(x)$ is Hermitian and positive semi-definite by construction.
Intuitively, $H(x)$ measures the collective ``error'' between the
observables $\{A_k\}$ and the target eigenvalues $\{x_k\}$: a data
point $x$ that is well-represented by the operators yields a small
ground state energy.

\begin{definition}[Quasi-coherent state]\label{def:qcs}
The \emph{quasi-coherent state} at $x$ is the ground state of $H(x)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ground_state}
  \ket{\psi(x)} = \argmin_{\ket{\phi},\,\|\phi\|=1}
  \dbraket{\phi}{H(x)}{\phi}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The map $x\mapsto\ket{\psi(x)}$ is a smooth embedding
$\R^d\hookrightarrow\CP^{n-1}$ wherever the ground state is
non-degenerate (i.e., the spectral gap $\Delta(x)>0$).  This map lifts
each market observation into the projective Hilbert space, endowing the
data manifold with quantum-mechanical geometry.

\begin{theorem}[Smoothness of the QCML embedding]\label{thm:smoothness}
Let $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^d$ be Hermitian operators and let $H(x)$ be the error
Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:hamiltonian}.  If the spectral gap
$\Delta(x) = E_1(x) - E_0(x) > 0$ for all $x$ in an open set
$U\subset\R^d$, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The ground state map $x \mapsto \ket{\psi(x)} \in \CP^{n-1}$ is
  $C^\infty$ on $U$.
\item The quantum metric tensor $g_{ab}(x)$ and Berry curvature
  $F_{ab}(x)$ are $C^\infty$ on $U$.
\item All geometric observables derived from $g_{ab}$ and $F_{ab}$
  (Christoffel symbols, Ricci scalar, Chern numbers) are $C^\infty$ on
  $U$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent The proof follows from the implicit function theorem applied to
the eigenvalue equation $H(x)\ket{\psi(x)} = E_0(x)\ket{\psi(x)}$: the
non-degeneracy condition $\Delta>0$ ensures that the ground state depends
smoothly on parameters.  See Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs} for details.

\paragraph{Operator learning.}  The operators $\{A_k\}$ are constructed
from the data covariance structure.  In the \emph{PCA-inspired} method,
we compute the principal components of the data matrix $X$, then scale
Pauli-basis operators by the corresponding eigenvalues:
$A_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}\,\sigma_k$, where $\sigma_k$ are tensor
products of Pauli matrices (for $n=4$, the two-qubit basis
$\{I\otimes I, I\otimes X, \ldots, Z\otimes Z\}$).  Alternative
methods include random Hermitian matrices and pure Pauli bases.

\subsection{Quantum Metric Tensor}

The pullback of the Fubini--Study metric \cite{provost_vallee1980} to $\R^d$ defines the
\emph{quantum metric tensor}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric}
  g_{ab}(x) = \mathrm{Re}\,\braket{\partial_a\psi}{\partial_b\psi}
  - \braket{\partial_a\psi}{\psi}\braket{\psi}{\partial_b\psi}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\partial_a\equiv\partial/\partial x^a$.  This is a real,
symmetric, positive semi-definite tensor that generalizes the Fisher
information metric to the Hilbert space setting.  It measures the
sensitivity of the quantum state to infinitesimal changes in the
data coordinates: large $g_{ab}$ indicates that nearby data points map
to distant states in $\CP^{n-1}$.

Partial derivatives are computed numerically via central finite
differences:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:central_diff}
  \ket{\partial_a\psi(x)} \approx
  \frac{\ket{\psi(x+\epsilon\, e_a)}-\ket{\psi(x-\epsilon\, e_a)}}{2\epsilon}\,,
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon=10^{-5}$.

\subsection{Berry Curvature}

The antisymmetric part of the quantum geometric tensor defines the
\emph{Berry curvature} \cite{berry1984,simon1983}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:berry}
  F_{ab}(x) = -2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\braket{\partial_a\psi}{\partial_b\psi}\,.
\end{equation}
This is a closed 2-form on $\R^d$ that encodes topological information.
While the metric tensor $g_{ab}$ is sensitive to parameterization, the
integral of $F_{ab}$ over closed surfaces yields topological invariants
(Chern numbers) that are integers \cite{nakahara2003} and therefore robust to smooth
deformations.

For two-dimensional subspaces, we also implement a \emph{plaquette}
(Wilson loop) method that is more numerically stable for computing Chern
numbers.  The Berry phase around a plaquette with corners
$x_{00}, x_{10}, x_{11}, x_{01}$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plaquette}
  \gamma = \mathrm{Im}\,\log\!\bigl(
  \braket{\psi_{00}}{\psi_{10}}
  \braket{\psi_{10}}{\psi_{11}}
  \braket{\psi_{11}}{\psi_{01}}
  \braket{\psi_{01}}{\psi_{00}}
  \bigr)\,,
\end{equation}
where the argument of the logarithm is the Wilson loop operator.  The
Berry curvature is then $F_{ab}\approx\gamma/\epsilon^2$.

\begin{theorem}[Chern number quantization]\label{thm:chern}
Let $S\subset\R^d$ be a closed, oriented 2-surface in parameter space
over which the ground state is non-degenerate ($\Delta>0$ on $S$).
Then the first Chern number
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chern_thm}
  C_1 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_S F_{ab}\, dx^a \wedge dx^b \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
is an integer.  In particular, $C_1$ is invariant under smooth
deformations of the Hamiltonian parameters that preserve $\Delta>0$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent This is a consequence of the Chern--Weil theorem applied to
the $U(1)$ line bundle over $S$ defined by the ground state
$\ket{\psi(x)}$; see Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs} for the proof.  The
quantization of $C_1$ is the mathematical basis for our claim that
Chern-number-based regime detection is \emph{topologically protected}:
small perturbations (noise) cannot change an integer.

\subsection{Spectral Gap}

The \emph{spectral gap} at $x$ is the difference between the two lowest
eigenvalues of the error Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
  \Delta(x) = E_1(x) - E_0(x)\,.
\end{equation}
A large gap indicates a well-defined, non-degenerate ground state and
hence stable geometry.  A closing gap signals the approach to a
\emph{quantum critical point}---in our financial analogy, a regime
transition \cite{sachdev2011}.  The spectral gap also controls the
accuracy of the adiabatic approximation: when $\Delta\to 0$, the system
is susceptible to non-adiabatic transitions between eigenstates.

\begin{theorem}[Curvature--gap bound]\label{thm:curvature_gap}
For any pair of indices $(a,b)$ with $a\neq b$, the Berry curvature is
bounded by the spectral gap:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature_gap_bound}
  |F_{ab}(x)| \;\leq\; \frac{K}{\Delta(x)^2}\,,
\end{equation}
where $K = 2\sum_{n\neq 0}
\bigl|\dbraket{n}{\partial_a H}{0}\bigr|\,
\bigl|\dbraket{n}{\partial_b H}{0}\bigr|$
depends on the Hamiltonian derivatives but not on the gap.
\end{theorem}

\noindent This bound follows from first-order perturbation theory for the
Berry connection (see Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs}).  It formalizes the
mechanism underlying curvature-based regime detection: as the spectral
gap $\Delta\to 0$ at a phase boundary, the Berry curvature can diverge
as $\Delta^{-2}$, producing the sharp spikes that our detectors identify.
The converse also holds qualitatively: in stable regimes where $\Delta$
is large, curvature fluctuations are suppressed.

\subsection{Quantum Fisher Information Susceptibility}

The \emph{QFI susceptibility} is defined as the trace of the quantum
metric tensor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfi}
  \chi(x) = \tr\bigl(g_{ab}(x)\bigr) = \sum_a g_{aa}(x)\,.
\end{equation}
This scalar quantity equals $\frac{1}{4}$ times the total quantum Fisher
information and measures the aggregate sensitivity of the quantum state
to parameter changes.  Near regime transitions, the market state becomes
maximally sensitive to perturbations, causing $\chi$ to spike---analogous
to divergent susceptibility at quantum critical points
\cite{zanardi2007}.

We further define the \emph{QFI pseudo-determinant} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qfi_det}
  \det^+(g) = \prod_{\lambda_i > \epsilon} \lambda_i\,,
\end{equation}
where $\{\lambda_i\}$ are the eigenvalues of $g_{ab}$ and
$\epsilon=10^{-10}$ filters near-zero eigenvalues for numerical
stability.  The pseudo-determinant measures the volume element of the
non-degenerate subspace of the data manifold: a collapsing
pseudo-determinant indicates that the effective dimensionality of the
market state is decreasing (approaching a phase boundary), while a
diverging pseudo-determinant indicates rapid expansion of the accessible
state space.

\begin{proposition}[QFI volume interpretation]\label{prop:qfi_volume}
The pseudo-determinant $\det^+(g)$ equals the squared Riemannian volume
element of the non-degenerate subspace of the data manifold.
Specifically, if $g_{ab}$ has $r$ positive eigenvalues
$\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_r > 0$, then
\begin{equation}
  \det^+(g) = \prod_{i=1}^r \lambda_i = \bigl(\mathrm{vol}_r\bigr)^2\,,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{vol}_r$ is the $r$-dimensional volume form induced by
the quantum metric restricted to the non-degenerate subspace.  Thus:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\det^+(g) \to 0$ indicates dimensional collapse of the accessible
  state space (approaching a phase boundary).
\item Large $\det^+(g)$ indicates the market state is exploring a
  high-dimensional region of quantum state space (stable, multi-factor
  regime).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is a direct consequence of the relationship between
the determinant of a metric tensor and the Riemannian volume element;
see Appendix~\ref{sec:proofs}.

\subsection{Quantum Distances}

The \emph{Fubini--Study distance} between two data points is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fs_distance}
  d_{\text{FS}}(x_1, x_2) = \arccos\!\bigl(|\braket{\psi(x_1)}{\psi(x_2)}|\bigr)\,.
\end{equation}
This gives a natural distance on the data manifold that respects the
quantum geometry: data points in the same regime have high fidelity
$|\braket{\psi(x_1)}{\psi(x_2)}|^2\approx 1$ and small $d_{\text{FS}}$,
while points in different regimes have low fidelity and large
$d_{\text{FS}}$.

\subsection{Higher Geometry: Christoffel Symbols and Ricci Scalar}

From the quantum metric $g_{ab}$, we compute the Levi-Civita connection
(Christoffel symbols):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:christoffel}
  \Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\nu}(x) = \frac{1}{2}\, g^{\sigma\rho}\!\left(
  \partial_\mu g_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\rho\mu}
  - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\right),
\end{equation}
and the Ricci scalar curvature:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ricci}
  R(x) = g^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}\,,
\end{equation}
where $R_{\mu\nu}=R^\sigma{}_{\mu\sigma\nu}$ is the Ricci tensor
obtained by contracting the Riemann curvature tensor.  We employ a
\emph{hierarchical finite difference} scheme with three levels of
step size ($\epsilon_{\text{metric}}=10^{-5}$,
$\epsilon_{\text{Christoffel}}=10^{-4}$,
$\epsilon_{\text{Ricci}}=10^{-3}$) to avoid differentiating numerical
noise.

Large $|R|$ indicates regions of high intrinsic curvature on the data
manifold, corresponding to nonlinear, unstable dynamics.

The geometric objects defined above---metric tensor $g_{ab}$, Berry
curvature $F_{ab}$, spectral gap $\Delta$, Ricci scalar $R$, QFI
matrix $\calF$---constitute a complete differential-geometric
description of the learned market manifold.  In subsequent sections, we
show that the dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:sde}) and observables
(Section~\ref{sec:observables}) derived from this geometry carry rich
information about market regime structure.

\section{Geometric Dynamics on the Market Manifold}\label{sec:sde}

Having endowed the data manifold with a Riemannian metric $g_{ab}$, we
construct a stochastic differential equation whose dynamics respect this
geometry.  This section formalizes how market evolution is a stochastic
process \emph{on the learned manifold}, not in an ambient Euclidean
space.  The diffusion tensor is not assumed but derived from the
geometry itself.

\subsection{Standard and Geometric SDEs}

A standard It\^{o} SDE in $\R^d$ takes the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standard_sde}
  dx^a = \mu^a\, dt + \sigma^a{}_b\, dW^b\,,
\end{equation}
where $\mu^a$ is the drift, $\sigma^a{}_b$ is the diffusion matrix, and
$W^b$ is a vector Wiener process.  In the geometric SDE, we replace the
constant (or state-dependent but Euclidean) diffusion with a
\emph{metric-induced} diffusion tensor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geometric_diffusion}
  \Sigma^{ab}(x) = \bigl(g^{-1}(x)\bigr)^{ab}\,,
\end{equation}
where $g^{-1}$ is the inverse metric (with eigenvalue regularization
$\lambda_i \mapsto \max(\lambda_i,\, \epsilon_{\text{reg}})$ for
numerical stability).  The intuition is that diffusion should be
\emph{faster} along directions where the quantum state is insensitive
to perturbations (small $g_{ab}$) and \emph{slower} along directions
where the state changes rapidly (large $g_{ab}$).

\subsection{Simulation Methods}

\paragraph{Euler--Maruyama.}
The simplest discretization is:
\begin{equation}
  x^a_{t+\Delta t} = x^a_t + \mu^a(x_t)\,\Delta t
  + (\Sigma^{1/2})^a{}_b(x_t)\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\;\xi^b\,,
\end{equation}
where $\xi^b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\Sigma^{1/2}$ is the matrix
square root (Cholesky factor) of $\Sigma$.

\paragraph{Milstein correction.}
For improved convergence, the Milstein scheme adds a correction term
involving derivatives of the diffusion coefficient.

\paragraph{Geodesic Brownian motion.}
On a Riemannian manifold, the natural stochastic process is Brownian
motion with respect to the Levi-Civita connection.  The drift receives
a geometric correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geodesic_drift}
  \mu^a_{\text{geo}}(x) = -\frac{1}{2}\,\Gamma^a_{bc}(x)\, g^{bc}(x)\,.
\end{equation}
This ensures that the process respects the curvature of the learned
manifold, concentrating trajectories in regions of high curvature
(high information content) and diffusing freely in flat regions.

\paragraph{Neural geometric SDE.}
We also consider a hybrid approach in which the drift and diffusion are
parameterized by neural networks but constrained to be compatible with
the learned metric.  The loss function is a Gaussian negative
log-likelihood penalizing deviations from observed transitions.

\section{Quantum Geometric Observables}\label{sec:observables}

The geometry defined in Sections~\ref{sec:geometry}--\ref{sec:sde}
induces a family of scalar observables that summarize the local and
global structure of the market manifold at each point in time.  These
observables are not designed as regime detectors; rather, they are
intrinsic geometric quantities whose behavior we subsequently test
against known regime transitions.

\subsection{Topological Invariants: Chern Numbers}

The \emph{first Chern number} of the line bundle associated with the
ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ over a closed 2-surface $\Sigma$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chern}
  C = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_\Sigma F_{ab}\, dx^a\wedge dx^b\,.
\end{equation}
For a closed surface, $C$ is an integer---a topological invariant
insensitive to smooth deformations of the surface or the Hamiltonian
parameters.  This integer-valuedness is the key property we exploit:
noise and volatility continuously deform the data manifold but cannot
change the Chern number unless a topological transition occurs.

In practice, we compute $C$ over rolling windows of market data using
either the plaquette (Wilson loop) method (Equation~\ref{eq:plaquette})
or trapezoidal integration of the Berry curvature.  The Chern number
characterizes the global topological charge of the market manifold
within each window; a change in $C$ between consecutive windows is a
necessary condition for a topological phase transition.

\subsection{Curvature and Metric Observables}

\subsubsection{Berry Phase Rate}

The \emph{Berry phase rate} measures the speed at which the system
traverses topological phase boundaries:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\gamma}(t) = |F_{01}(x_t) - F_{01}(x_{t-1})|\,,
\end{equation}
where $F_{01}$ is the Berry curvature in the first two principal
component directions.  Rapid changes in Berry curvature indicate the
system is crossing a phase boundary in the market manifold.

\subsubsection{Metric Condition Number}

The condition number of the quantum metric tensor,
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(g) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(g)}{\lambda_{\min}(g)}\,,
\end{equation}
measures the anisotropy of the data manifold.  High $\kappa$ indicates
the manifold is anisotropically stretched---a geometric signature of
regime transitions, which typically affect some market factors more than
others.

\subsection{Quantum State Observables}

\subsubsection{Spectral Gap}

By analogy with quantum phase transitions, where the spectral gap
$\Delta=E_1-E_0$ closes at critical points \cite{sachdev2011}, we
hypothesize that $\Delta(t)$ narrows before financial regime
transitions.  A \emph{gap collapse} is detected when
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(t) < \bar{\Delta}_t - k\,\hat{\sigma}_{\Delta,t}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\Delta}_t$ and $\hat{\sigma}_{\Delta,t}$ are the rolling
mean and standard deviation over a window of size $W$, and $k=2$
is the collapse threshold in standard deviations.  A closing spectral
gap signals the approach to a quantum critical point of the market
manifold---a regime boundary where the ground state becomes ill-defined.

\subsubsection{Ground State Energy}

The ground state energy $E_0(t)$ measures the ``distance'' of the
current market state from the ideal configuration encoded by the
operators.  We detect stress events when
\begin{equation}
  E_0(t) > \bar{E}_{0,t} + k\,\hat{\sigma}_{E_0,t}\,.
\end{equation}
Higher $E_0$ indicates that the market has moved to a region of feature
space poorly represented by the learned operators---a geometric analog
of distributional shift, reflecting departure from the manifold's
low-energy basin.

\subsubsection{Fidelity Decay Rate (Loschmidt Echo Analog)}

The \emph{quantum fidelity} between consecutive states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fidelity}
  \mathcal{F}(t, t+\delta) = \bigl|\braket{\psi(x_t)}{\psi(x_{t+\delta})}\bigr|^2\,,
\end{equation}
measures the rate of quantum state evolution.  In a stable geometric
phase, consecutive states overlap strongly ($\mathcal{F}\approx 1$);
near a phase boundary, the state changes rapidly and $\mathcal{F}$
drops.  This is analogous to the Loschmidt echo in quantum dynamics.
Instability is flagged when $\mathcal{F}(t)$ drops below
$\bar{\mathcal{F}}_t - k\,\hat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},t}$.

\subsubsection{Multi-Lag Fidelity}

Rather than a single lag $\delta$, we compute fidelity at multiple lags
$\delta\in\{1,3,5,10\}$ and form a weighted average infidelity:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mathcal{I}}(t) = \sum_j w_j\,
  \bigl(1-|\braket{\psi(x_t)}{\psi(x_{t-\delta_j})}|^2\bigr)\,,
\end{equation}
with weights $[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]$ favoring short lags.  Short lags
detect fast crises; long lags detect gradual transitions.  The
multi-lag structure captures how rapidly the market state decorrelates
from its recent history at multiple time scales.

\subsection{Information-Theoretic Observables}

The QFI susceptibility $\chi(x)=\tr(g_{ab}(x))$ and pseudo-determinant
$\det^+(g)$ (defined in Section~\ref{sec:geometry}) are
information-theoretic summaries of the quantum metric.  The
susceptibility measures aggregate state sensitivity to parameter
changes, spiking near regime transitions analogously to divergent
susceptibility at quantum critical points \cite{zanardi2007}.  The
pseudo-determinant measures the effective dimensionality of the quantum
state; its collapse signals that fewer market factors are actively
driving dynamics---a hallmark of crisis contagion.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Consensus and Composite Score}

\subsubsection{Multi-Scale Chern Consensus}

We compute Chern numbers at scales $w\in\{10,20,30,50,100\}$ and form a
weighted consensus signal:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{cons}}(t) = \sum_{i} w_i\,
  \frac{|\Delta C_t^{(w_i)}|}{\hat{\sigma}_{\Delta C}^{(w_i)}+\epsilon}\,,
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are normalized scale weights.

\subsubsection{Composite Score}

All indicators are combined into a composite regime stress score.  Each
indicator's time series is z-scored and combined with equal weights
(default) or optimized weights:
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\text{comp}}(t) = \sum_i w_i\, \tilde{z}_i(t)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{z}_i$ is the z-scored indicator (with sign flip for
spectral gap and fidelity, where lower values indicate more stress).

\section{Regime Detection as Geometric Phase Change}\label{sec:detection}

A \emph{regime}, in our geometric framework, is a connected region of
the data manifold with approximately constant quantum metric spectrum
and topological charge.  A \emph{regime transition} is a trajectory
$x(t)$ that crosses between regions with distinct curvature signatures
or Chern numbers.  The observables defined in
Section~\ref{sec:observables} are direct probes of this geometric
change; in this section, we formalize how they are used for detection.

\subsection{Rolling Chern Number and Transition Detection}

\begin{criterion}[Regime transition]\label{crit:transition}
A topological regime transition is declared at time $t$ if the rolling
Chern number satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transition_criterion}
  |\Delta C_t| = |C_{[t-w,t]} - C_{[t-2w,t-w]}| > \tau\,,
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the window size and $\tau$ is an adaptive threshold.
Events satisfying this criterion are classified as
\textsf{REGIME\_CHANGE}; events with $|\Delta C_t|\leq\tau$ but
$\max|F_{ab}|>3\sigma$ are classified as \textsf{EXTREME\_EVENT}; all
others are \textsf{NORMAL\_FLUCTUATION}.
\end{criterion}

\subsection{Adaptive Thresholds and Confirmation}

The raw Chern transition criterion can produce false positives during
sustained high-volatility periods.  We implement several improvements:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Adaptive thresholds.}  The threshold $\tau$ is modulated
  by the ratio of short-term to long-term volatility of
  $\Delta C$:
  \[
    \tau_t = \tau_0\,\bigl(1+\alpha\,
    \max(0,\,\sigma^{\text{short}}_t/\sigma^{\text{long}}_t - 1)\bigr)
    \cdot\sigma^{\text{long}}_t\,.
  \]
  In high-volatility regimes, $\tau_t$ increases, reducing false
  positives.

\item \textbf{Quantile thresholds.}  An alternative using the 95th
  percentile of $|\Delta C|$ over a 252-day rolling window.

\item \textbf{Multi-scale consensus.}  Chern numbers are computed at
  multiple window sizes $w\in\{10,20,30,50\}$.  A regime transition is
  confirmed only when at least 2 of 4 scales agree.  Scale weights are
  dynamically adjusted, with default allocation
  $[0.15, 0.30, 0.30, 0.25]$ favoring medium time scales.

\item \textbf{Spike confirmation (persistence filter).}  Raw spike
  detections must persist for $\geq 2$ consecutive days before
  confirmation, filtering single-day noise.

\item \textbf{Quantization.}  Since $C$ is theoretically an integer for
  closed surfaces, we apply soft quantization
  $\tilde{C} = (1-s)\,C + s\,\mathrm{round}(C)$ with strength
  $s\in[0,1]$ to reduce estimation noise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Detection Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:detection} summarizes the full detection pipeline.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Geometric Regime Detection Pipeline}\label{alg:detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Feature matrix $X\in\R^{T\times d}$, Hilbert dimension $n$,
  window sizes $\{w_i\}$, threshold $\tau$
\Ensure Regime transition list $\mathcal{T}$, composite scores $Z(t)$
\State \textbf{Preprocessing:} Standardize $X$; PCA to $p$
  components; $L^2$-normalize rows
\State \textbf{Operator learning:} Fit $\{A_k\}_{k=1}^p$ from
  covariance eigenstructure
\For{each time point $t=1,\ldots,T$}
  \State Compute $H(x_t)$ via Equation~\ref{eq:hamiltonian}
  \State Solve $\ket{\psi(x_t)}=$ ground state of $H(x_t)$
  \State Record $E_0(t)$, $\Delta(t)$, $\mathcal{F}(t,t-1)$
\EndFor
\For{each window size $w_i$}
  \State Compute rolling Chern $C^{(w_i)}(t)$ via plaquette method
\EndFor
\State Compute Berry phase rate $\dot{\gamma}(t)$
\State Compute QFI susceptibility $\chi(t)=\tr(g_{ab}(x_t))$
\State Compute QFI pseudo-determinant $\det^+(g(x_t))$
\State Compute metric condition number $\kappa(g(x_t))$
\State Form composite score $Z_{\text{comp}}(t)$ from z-scored
  indicators
\State Detect transitions $\mathcal{T}=\{t: |\Delta C_t|>\tau
  \text{ and persistence} \geq 2\}$
\State \Return $\mathcal{T}$, $Z_{\text{comp}}(t)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Statistical Testing Protocol}

For each method and each crisis, we apply the following tests:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Welch's $t$-test} for the difference in mean regime
  scores between crisis and non-crisis windows.  Significance at
  $\alpha=0.05$ with Holm--Bonferroni step-down correction across 11
  QCML method comparisons.

\item \textbf{Cohen's $d$} effect size: $d=(\bar{x}_{\text{post}}-
  \bar{x}_{\text{pre}})/s_{\text{pooled}}$.  We target $d>0.5$ (medium
  effect) as the minimum for a practically meaningful signal, with
  $d>0.8$ (large effect) as the goal.

\item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence interval} ($n=10{,}000$ resamples)
  on the mean score difference.  The interval must exclude zero.

\item \textbf{Permutation test} ($n=5{,}000$ permutations) to assess
  whether the observed $t$-statistic exceeds the null distribution.

\item \textbf{Bayes factor} (parametric): $\text{BF}_{10}>10$ for
  ``strong evidence'' in favor of $H_1$.
\end{enumerate}

A method is declared to \emph{beat} the Random Forest baseline if it
achieves a significantly larger mean Cohen's $d$ across crises (paired
Welch's $t$-test on the $d$-values, $p<0.05$).

\subsection{Method Correlation Analysis}\label{subsec:method_corr}

With 11 QCML methods, redundancy among observables is a concern.  We
compute pairwise Spearman correlations across all QCML score time series
and perform PCA to quantify the number of independent signals.

PCA of the 11 QCML score time series reveals approximately three
independent signals: the first three principal components explain
$58.8\%$ of total variance (33.4\%, 14.6\%, 10.8\%), with subsequent
components contributing $< 10\%$ each.  This indicates that while
QCML observables are correlated---as expected, since they derive from
the same geometric structure---they capture at least three distinct
aspects of manifold geometry (curvature-related, metric-related, and
fidelity-related signals).  The Spearman correlation analysis (not
shown) confirms that Berry Phase Rate and QFI Determinant are the
least redundant pair ($\rho < 0.3$), supporting their joint use in
ensemble methods (Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid}).

Additional experiments---TDA comparison, learned operator exploration,
noise robustness, and SDE empirical validation---are presented in
Appendix~\ref{sec:supplementary}.  In brief: QCML outperforms
persistent homology TDA ($\bar{d} = 0.93$ vs.\ $0.58$); end-to-end
learned operators fail due to gradient collapse (validating PCA-based
construction); Berry Phase Rate and QFI Determinant show strong noise
robustness while RF degrades $61\%$ at SNR${}= 3$\,dB; and the
geometric SDE produces return distributions closer to reality than
isotropic diffusion (K-S $0.27$ vs.\ $0.32$).


\subsection{Multi-Observable Geometric Fusion}\label{subsec:fusion}

The preceding experiments establish that the top three QCML observables---Berry
phase rate ($d=0.99$), QFI pseudo-determinant ($d=0.86$), and multi-lag fidelity
($d=0.84$)---each dominate Random Forest on complementary subsets of crises.
The per-crisis oracle maximum across these three methods is $d=1.42$, far
exceeding RF's $d=1.10$.  Moreover, the methods exhibit low mutual
correlation (Spearman $\rho = 0.18$--$0.46$; three PCA components explain
only 59\% of variance), suggesting that \emph{score-level fusion} should
capture complementary geometric information.

We test this hypothesis with two fusion architectures, both evaluated through
the same \texttt{evaluate\_method()} pipeline used for all 16 methods.

\subsubsection{Phase A: Score-Level Fusion}

Each sub-detector produces a causal expanding z-score time series.
The \textbf{FusedQCMLDetector} combines these via one of three strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Max fusion}: $s(t) = \max\bigl(z_{\mathrm{Berry}}(t),\,
  z_{\mathrm{QFI}}(t),\, z_{\mathrm{Fidelity}}(t)\bigr)$---selects
  the strongest geometric signal at each timestep.
\item \textbf{Weighted mean}: $s(t) = \sum_i w_i\, z_i(t)$ with
  Optuna-optimized weights.
\item \textbf{Rank mean}: rank each method's scores independently,
  average ranks, then z-score the result.
\end{enumerate}

Hyperparameters (fusion strategy, Hilbert dimension, PCA components,
operator method, rolling window, minimum expanding window) are
optimized via Optuna TPE with 200 trials, maximizing the median
rank-normalized Cohen's $d$ across 12-fold leave-one-crisis-out
cross-validation---the same protocol used for RF training.

The best configuration is \textbf{weighted mean} fusion with weights
$w_{\mathrm{MultiLag}} = 0.47$, $w_{\mathrm{QFI}} = 0.43$,
$w_{\mathrm{Berry}} = 0.09$ (Hilbert dimension $h=12$,
PCA-inspired operators, rolling window~10, minimum expanding
window~80), achieving
\textbf{median $d = 1.67$} across 12~crises---a 69\% improvement over
the individual best (Berry phase rate, $d=0.99$) and 48\% above RF
($d=1.13$).  The low Berry weight is notable: the QFI and
fidelity observables already capture most of the curvature information that
Berry phase rate provides, so adding it yields only marginal gain.
The fused detector achieves $d > 1.7$ on
five crises where RF scores $d < 0.5$ (Volmageddon, SVB, Quant
Meltdown, Debt Downgrade, Rate Hike).

\subsubsection{Phase B: Operator-Optimized Fusion}

The \textbf{GeometryOptimizedDetector} takes fusion deeper by optimizing
the Hermitian operators themselves.  Starting from PCA-inspired basis
operators $\{A_k\}$, we introduce per-operator scale factors
$\alpha_k \in [0.1, 5.0]$ that modify the error Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
H(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(\alpha_k A_k - x_k I\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to learning a diagonal metric on operator space,
changing the curvature of the energy landscape in each PCA dimension.
From this single optimized geometry, we extract five observables:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Berry curvature rate of change,
\item log pseudo-determinant of the quantum metric,
\item multi-lag fidelity ($\ell = 1, 3, 5, 10$),
\item inverse spectral gap of the error Hamiltonian,
\item log condition number of the quantum metric.
\end{enumerate}
Each observable is z-scored causally, then fused via learned weights
$\{w_j\}_{j=1}^5$.  The total parameter count is 14 (8 operator
scales + 5 fusion weights + 1 rolling window), well within Optuna
TPE's effective range.

Unlike the gradient-based Cayley map approach
(Appendix~\ref{app:learned_ops}), black-box optimization avoids
eigendecomposition gradients and the associated mode collapse.

The optimized geometry achieves \textbf{median $d = 1.79$} across
12~crises (200 Optuna trials, 11~minutes), surpassing both Phase~A
and RF.  The learned operator scales concentrate curvature in PCA
dimensions~0 and~2 ($\alpha_0 = 2.4$, $\alpha_2 = 4.4$) while
suppressing dimensions~3--4 ($\alpha_3 = 0.26$, $\alpha_4 = 0.13$),
indicating that the geometry allocates sensitivity to volatile market
factors.  Among the five observables, Berry curvature rate receives
the largest fusion weight ($w_1 = 0.79$), followed by the inverse
spectral gap ($w_4 = 0.70$) and QFI determinant ($w_2 = 0.62$).
Phase~B wins on 8 of 12~crises against RF, including the 2008~GFC
($d = 2.09$ vs.\ RF $d = 1.74$) and COVID-19 ($d = 2.11$ vs.\ RF
$d = 1.20$).

\subsubsection{Discussion}

At inference time, neither fusion architecture uses crisis labels: the
geometry is determined entirely by the data manifold structure.
Hyperparameter calibration optimizes across all 12 crises
simultaneously (maximizing median Cohen's~$d$).
A nested leave-one-crisis-out analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:overfitting}) validates that the calibrated
parameters generalize to unseen crises.  The fused QCML
system therefore remains unsupervised in the same sense that any
method with calibrated hyperparameters is unsupervised.

The fusion results demonstrate that the geometric observables
carry genuinely non-redundant information about regime transitions,
and that even a simple weighted combination converts complementarity
into aggregate detection power exceeding the supervised baseline.
Phase~A (score-level, median $d = 1.67$) and Phase~B
(operator-optimized, median $d = 1.79$) exhibit complementary
strengths: Phase~A dominates on gradual crises (Rate Hike, SVB)
while Phase~B excels on rapid crashes (GFC $d = 2.09$, COVID
$d = 2.11$, Volmageddon $d = 2.02$), suggesting that the two
architectures could themselves be combined in future work.

\subsection{Generalization Analysis}\label{sec:overfitting}

The fusion results in Section~\ref{subsec:fusion} were obtained by
optimizing hyperparameters on all 12 crises simultaneously.  While
this is standard practice for unsupervised calibration (no crisis
labels are used at inference time), it risks overfitting the
hyperparameters to the particular set of crises.  We quantify this
risk through nested cross-validation and temporal out-of-sample
re-optimization.

\subsubsection{Nested Leave-One-Crisis-Out Validation}

For each of the 12 crises, we hold it out entirely, re-run Phase~A
Optuna optimization (30 trials) on the remaining 11 crises, and
evaluate the best configuration on the held-out crisis.  This yields
an unbiased estimate of generalization performance, since the held-out
crisis is never seen during hyperparameter selection.

The unbiased held-out median $d = 1.09$ represents a 35.6\%
reduction from the calibration estimate ($d = 1.69$), confirming
moderate overfitting.  Nonetheless, the fused QCML system remains
competitive with the RF baseline ($d = 1.15$), with the gap
falling within the standard error of both estimates.  Per-fold
results range from $d = 0.07$ (2022 rates, where rate-driven
regimes differ structurally from equity crises) to $d = 1.95$
(2011 debt downgrade), reflecting crisis heterogeneity rather
than systematic failure.

\subsubsection{Temporal Out-of-Sample Re-Optimization}

Section~\ref{subsec:oos} reported temporal OOS performance using
parameters that had been calibrated on all 12 crises, including the
three post-2020 test crises.  Here, we re-optimize Phase~A using only
the nine pre-2020 crises (2007--2019) and evaluate on the three
post-2020 crises with frozen parameters.

The re-optimized fused QCML achieves post-2020 median $d = 1.82$,
compared to $d = 1.72$ from the original calibration and $d = 0.88$
for the Random Forest baseline.  Remarkably, the re-optimized system
performs \emph{better} on post-2020 crises than its pre-2020 training
performance ($d = 1.70$), yielding a negative overfitting gap of
$-0.12$.  Per-crisis results are consistent: COVID $d = 1.82$,
2022 rate hikes $d = 1.95$, SVB $d = 1.79$.  This strong temporal
generalization confirms that the geometric fusion captures genuine
topological crisis signatures rather than crisis-specific artifacts.

\subsubsection{Parameter Stability}

Across the 12 nested LOCO folds, we examine whether the optimal
hyperparameters are stable or crisis-dependent:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Categorical parameters} (fusion method, Hilbert
    dimension, operator method): we report the agreement rate---the
    fraction of folds selecting the same value.  High agreement
    ($>80\%$) indicates robust calibration.
  \item \textbf{Continuous parameters} (fusion weights, rolling
    window): we report the coefficient of variation (CV).  Low CV
    ($<30\%$) indicates stable optimization.
\end{itemize}

Across folds, the mean categorical agreement rate is 52\%:
\texttt{operator\_method} shows 58\% agreement on
\texttt{pca\_inspired}, \texttt{fusion\_method} shows 50\% agreement
on \texttt{weighted\_mean}, and \texttt{hilbert\_dim} is evenly split
between 8 and 12 (42\% each).  Continuous fusion weights exhibit
high variability: $w_{\text{berry}}$ CV${}= 82\%$,
$w_{\text{qfi}}$ CV${}= 74\%$, $w_{\text{multilag}}$ CV${}= 86\%$.
This moderate instability suggests that score-level fusion
hyperparameters are sensitive to the particular crisis mix, motivating
the complementarity framework (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) over
rigid parameter optimization.

\subsubsection{Improved Individual Baselines}

The Optuna optimization consistently identified
\texttt{pca\_inspired} operators as superior to random initialization.
An extended sensitivity analysis
(Section~\ref{subsec:extended_sensitivity}) sweeping operator method,
rolling window, Hilbert dimension, and PCA components confirms this:
with optimized hyperparameters, all three individual QCML methods
\emph{exceed} the RF baseline without requiring any fusion.
Specifically, QFI Determinant improves from $d = 0.93$ to $d = 1.42$
(+52\%), Berry Phase Rate from $d = 0.93$ to $d = 1.31$ (+41\%),
and Multi-Lag Fidelity from $d = 0.84$ to $d = 1.26$ (+49\%),
all surpassing the RF baseline of $d = 1.13$.  The
\texttt{pca\_inspired} operator method accounts for the largest
improvement, with Multi-Lag Fidelity showing the strongest effect
(+0.23 mean $d$ advantage over random operators).

\subsubsection{Causal Hyperparameter Optimization}\label{subsec:causal_hpo}

The preceding analyses optimized hyperparameters using full crisis data,
risking lookahead bias.  To validate causal performance, we re-optimized
all three QCML methods under a strict causal constraint: PCA, scaling,
and quantum operators are fitted \emph{only} on pre-crisis data
($t < t_{\text{crisis}}$), then applied forward in time to detect the
regime transition.  This mirrors real-world deployment where future
information is unavailable.

We employed Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler
for intelligent hyperparameter search over the space: \texttt{hilbert\_dim}
$\in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$, \texttt{n\_pca\_components} $\in \{8, 10, 15, 20\}$,
\texttt{operator\_method} $\in \{\text{random}, \text{pca\_inspired}\}$,
and \texttt{rolling\_window} $\in \{10, 20, 30\}$.  The objective
function maximized median Cohen's $d$ across 12 crises under leave-one-crisis-out
validation, with 200 TPE trials per method (Phase~A).  An optional
Phase~B refined the expanding window refit interval on top of Phase~A
configurations (150 trials).

\textbf{Causal-Optimal Configurations Differ Substantially.}  Under the
causal constraint, optimal hyperparameters diverge from non-causal
settings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rolling window}: All methods converge to \texttt{rolling\_window}
    $= 30$ under causal optimization, versus 10--20 in non-causal settings.
    Longer windows provide stability when operators are calibrated on limited
    pre-crisis data.
  \item \textbf{Operator method}: \texttt{pca\_inspired} dominates for
    QFI Determinant ($+0.30$ mean $d$ over random) and Multi-Lag Fidelity
    ($+0.34$), confirming that principal component alignment is critical
    when training data is restricted.
  \item \textbf{Hilbert dimension}: Berry Phase Rate prefers $h=16$
    (higher than non-causal $h=8$), while QFI and Multi-Lag Fidelity
    stabilize at $h=8$--$h=12$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Statistical Validation.}  We evaluated six conditions across
12 crises: (1)~original non-causal defaults with causal fitting,
(2)~Optuna Phase~A causal-optimized, (2b)~Phase~B expanding window,
(3)~expanding window with 20-step refit, (4)~expanding window with
30-step refit, and (5)~Random Forest baseline (leave-one-crisis-out).
Results (Figure~\ref{fig:optuna_6condition}) demonstrate that causal
optimization significantly improves performance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{QFI Determinant} (Phase~A): mean $d = 2.295$, Wilcoxon
    $p = 0.0105$ (${}^*$, significant at $\alpha = 0.05$).
  \item \textbf{Multi-Lag Fidelity} (Phase~A): mean $d = 2.285$, Wilcoxon
    $p = 0.0046$ (${}^{**}$, highly significant).
  \item \textbf{Berry Phase Rate} (Phase~B): mean $d = 1.961$, Wilcoxon
    $p = 0.0647$ (${}^\dagger$, marginal significance).
\end{itemize}

Both QFI Determinant and Multi-Lag Fidelity \emph{statistically exceed}
the RF baseline under causal optimization.  Friedman ranking across all
conditions confirms significant method differences ($\chi^2 = 20.8$,
$p = 0.001$).  Figure~\ref{fig:optuna_heatmap} shows per-crisis
performance heatmaps, revealing that QCML methods excel in fast crashes
(2008 GFC, 2020 COVID) while RF dominates slow-building crises
(2018 Vol Spike).

\textbf{Expanding Window Trade-off.}  Phase~B expanding window
optimization (periodic refitting with growing data) improves Berry
Phase Rate ($+0.25$ mean $d$) but \emph{degrades} QFI Determinant
($-0.43$) and Multi-Lag Fidelity ($-0.48$).  This asymmetry reflects
differential sensitivity to operator recalibration: Berry curvature
benefits from adaptive updates, while QFI-based methods suffer from
parameter instability during volatile periods.
Figure~\ref{fig:optuna_phase_ab} illustrates this trade-off across
all 12 crises.

The Friedman test ranking (Figure~\ref{fig:optuna_ranking}) places
QFI Determinant (Phase~A) as the top unsupervised method, followed
closely by Multi-Lag Fidelity (Phase~A).  This validates the
causal optimization framework: with proper hyperparameter tuning
under realistic constraints, QCML geometric indicators achieve
competitive and statistically significant regime detection performance.

\subsection{Multi-Asset Generalization}\label{subsec:multi_asset}

A key question for any regime detection framework is whether it
generalizes beyond the single asset (SPY) used for calibration.  We
test whether the learned geometric observables detect crises in
five broad-market ETFs spanning US large-cap (SPY), technology

blue-chip (DIA) equities---each analyzed independently using its own
price-derived feature matrix.

For each asset--crisis pair, we construct the feature matrix from the

QCML geometry with the causal-optimized hyperparameters from

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Conventional vs.\ Novel Crises}

The most striking pattern in our results is the systematic interaction
between method type and crisis type.  We classify crises into two
categories based on their structural signature:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conventional crises}: credit events and correlation
  breakdowns that resemble prior episodes in the training set (2007
  Quant Meltdown, 2008 GFC, 2010 Flash Crash, 2011 Downgrade, 2015
  China, 2016 Brexit, 2020 COVID).  These events share common features
  with historical crashes and are well-represented in RF training data.

\item \textbf{Novel crises}: structurally unfamiliar events
  involving new market mechanisms (2018 Volmageddon short-vol blowup,
  2018 Fed Selloff, 2019 Repo rate spike, 2022 rate regime shift, 2023
  SVB bank run).  These events have few or no historical precedents in
  the training set.
\end{itemize}

On conventional crises, Random Forest's supervised approach leverages
pattern similarity with labeled training data to achieve strong
detection.  On novel crises, the lack of similar training examples
degrades RF performance, while QCML methods---detecting geometric and
topological deformations of the market state space---respond to the
\emph{structural novelty itself} rather than pattern similarity.

The temporal out-of-sample validation (Section~\ref{subsec:oos})
provides direct evidence: when RF is trained exclusively on pre-2020
crises and tested on post-2020 events, its performance degrades on
structurally dissimilar crises while QCML methods maintain their
detection power.

\subsection{The Complementarity Thesis}

Our central finding is that topological and supervised detectors are
\emph{complementary} rather than competing approaches.  Three lines of
evidence support this thesis:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bimodal performance}: QCML dominates on novel crises
  (e.g., QFI Determinant $d=2.21$ on SVB vs.\ RF $d=0.14$), while RF
  dominates on conventional ones (e.g., RF $d=2.09$ on 2008 vs.\ QCML
  Chern $d=0.25$).  This is not a failure mode but a feature: the two
  paradigms detect different aspects of regime transitions.

\item \textbf{Temporal robustness}: In the out-of-sample validation
  (Section~\ref{subsec:oos}), QCML methods show minimal degradation
  from in-sample to OOS performance, while RF degrades substantially
  on structurally novel test crises.

\item \textbf{Ensemble improvement}: The hybrid ensemble
  (Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid}) achieves higher mean $d$ than either
  QCML or RF alone, confirming that the two signal types carry
  non-redundant information about regime transitions.
\end{enumerate}

This complementarity has a natural geometric interpretation.  Random
Forest detects crises by \emph{pattern matching} against known crisis
signatures in \emph{flat} feature space---projecting each window into
$\R^d$ and comparing it to labeled training examples.  The
geometry-induced observables detect crises by measuring
\emph{curvature, topology, and metric deformation} of the learned
manifold---a fundamentally different signal that responds to structural
change in the \emph{curved} market state space regardless of whether
such change has been observed before.

\subsection{Numerical Stability}

All geometric quantities involve finite-difference derivatives of
quantum states obtained by diagonalizing the error Hamiltonian.  Two
sources of numerical instability merit attention:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Finite-difference step size.}  We employ a hierarchical
  scheme: $\epsilon_{\text{metric}}=10^{-5}$ for the quantum metric,
  $\epsilon_{\text{Christoffel}}=10^{-4}$ for Christoffel symbols, and
  $\epsilon_{\text{Ricci}}=10^{-3}$ for the Ricci tensor.  Each level
  uses $10\times$ coarser step size to avoid differentiating numerical
  noise from the previous level.

\item \textbf{Spectral gap degeneracy.}  When $\Delta(x)\to 0$, the
  ground state becomes ill-defined.  We regularize the inverse metric
  via eigenvalue clamping:
  $\lambda_i\mapsto\max(\lambda_i,\epsilon_{\text{reg}})$.  The spectral
  gap itself serves as a diagnostic: regions where $\Delta<10^{-8}$
  are flagged and excluded from Chern integration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Non-Integer Chern Numbers}

In theory, the Chern number is an integer for closed surfaces.  In
practice, our Chern numbers are computed over \emph{open} windows of
market data and are therefore non-integer.  The deviation from
integrality is itself informative: small deviations indicate a
well-defined topological phase, while large deviations may indicate
proximity to a phase transition.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

The dominant cost is the eigendecomposition of $H(x)$, which is
$O(n^3)$ per data point, where $n$ is the Hilbert space dimension.
For $n=8$ and $T=5{,}000$ trading days, the full pipeline requires
approximately 45 minutes on a single CPU core.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}

We evaluate the sensitivity of the top three QCML methods to the
two primary hyperparameters: the Hilbert space dimension
$n\in\{4,8,16\}$ and the number of PCA components
$d\in\{5,10,15,20\}$.  Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} shows the mean
Cohen's~$d$ across four representative crises for each $(n,d)$
combination.

The Berry phase rate and QFI determinant both achieve their strongest
performance at $n=8$, with mean $d=1.39$ (Berry, $d_{\text{PCA}}=10$)
and $d=1.45$ (QFI, $d_{\text{PCA}}=15$) respectively.  Both methods
degrade significantly at $n=16$, suggesting that the larger state
space overfits the finite sample.  The multi-lag fidelity detector is
the most robust to hyperparameter choice, with a coefficient of
variation of only 24.6\%.

\subsubsection{Extended Sensitivity: Operator Method and Rolling Window}%
\label{subsec:extended_sensitivity}

The original analysis varied only Hilbert dimension and PCA components
while holding the operator initialization method (\texttt{random})
and rolling window ($w=20$) fixed.  The fused Optuna optimization
(Section~\ref{subsec:fusion}) consistently identified
\texttt{pca\_inspired} operators as superior to \texttt{random}.  To
validate this finding independently of the fusion framework, we extend
the grid to include:
$n \in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$,
$d_{\text{PCA}} \in \{8, 10, 15, 20\}$,
operator method $\in \{\text{random}, \text{pca\_inspired}\}$, and
rolling window $w \in \{10, 20, 30\}$---yielding 96 configurations per
method.  A two-stage protocol first screens on 4 representative crises
(Stage~1), then validates the top 5 configurations on all 12 crises
(Stage~2) with full bootstrap statistics.

\subsection{Open Problems}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Operator learning.}  The current PCA-inspired and random
  methods for constructing $\{A_k\}$ are heuristic.  A principled
  approach would learn operators that maximize the discriminative power
  of the Chern number for regime detection.

\item \textbf{Real-time streaming.}  Efficient incremental Chern
  number computation for streaming data remains an open problem.

\item \textbf{Higher-dimensional topology.}  Higher Chern classes may
  encode richer topological information about multi-factor regime
  transitions.

\item \textbf{Cross-asset generalization.}  Our temporal
  out-of-sample validation provides partial evidence of generalization
  (to novel crises within equities), but whether the topological signal
  persists across asset classes remains to be established.

\item \textbf{Real-time hybrid deployment.}  The complementarity
  thesis motivates a production system combining QCML and supervised
  detectors, with the dynamic switching mechanism
  (Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid}) as a starting point.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The central contribution of this paper is the construction of a learned
Riemannian manifold for financial markets whose geometric and
topological properties encode regime structure.  Regime
detection---far from being the starting point---emerges as a natural
consequence: the manifold's observables (Berry phase rate, QFI
pseudo-determinant, Chern numbers, fidelity measures) change sharply at
known crisis boundaries, achieving large effect sizes that exceed the
supervised Random Forest baseline.

Our strongest findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Multi-observable fusion exceeds supervised baselines.}
  Optuna-optimized weighted fusion of the three top geometric
  observables achieves median $d = 1.67$ across 12 crises---48\%
  above the Random Forest baseline ($d = 1.13$)---without using any
  crisis labels at inference time
  (Section~\ref{subsec:fusion}).

\item \textbf{Causal regime detection achieves $d > 2.0$.}
  Under a strictly causal protocol that prevents all lookahead bias,
  individual geometric observables reach $d = 2.30$ (QFI Determinant)
  and $d = 2.29$ (Multi-Lag Fidelity), demonstrating that the geometric
  signal is genuine and not an artifact of temporal leakage
  (Section~\ref{subsec:causal_hpo}).

\item \textbf{Multi-asset generalization.}
  All three QCML methods maintain $d > 1.0$ on five broad-market ETFs
  (SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA, DIA) with Friedman $\chi^2 = 35.0$,
  $p < 0.0001$, confirming that the geometric framework captures
  universal features of regime transitions rather than SPY-specific
  patterns (Section~\ref{subsec:multi_asset}).

\item \textbf{Complementary to supervised detection.}
  On novel crises (SVB, COVID), QCML achieves $d > 1.7$ where RF
  scores $d < 0.5$.  A hybrid ensemble nearly doubles out-of-sample
  $d$ relative to RF alone ($d = 0.60$ vs.\ $d = 0.31$),
  confirming non-redundant information content
  (Sections~\ref{subsec:oos},~\ref{subsec:hybrid}).

\item \textbf{Individual methods exceed RF with proper initialization.}
  With PCA-inspired operators and tuned rolling windows, all three
  individual QCML methods exceed RF \emph{without} fusion: QFI
  Determinant $d = 1.42$, Berry Phase Rate $d = 1.31$, Multi-Lag
  Fidelity $d = 1.26$ (vs.\ RF $d = 1.13$)
  (Section~\ref{subsec:extended_sensitivity}).
\end{enumerate}

The practical implication is clear: the learned market manifold provides
a principled geometric framework whose curvature, topology, and metric
properties not only complement but \emph{exceed} supervised pattern
matching on regime detection.  The geometric signal is robust across
assets, causal protocols, and novel crisis types.  Future work will
pursue real-time hybrid deployment, cross-asset joint geometry (fixed
income, commodities, FX), learned operators via end-to-end
optimization, and higher-dimensional topological invariants for
multi-factor regime classification.

\section{Trading Strategy and Backtesting Framework}\label{sec:trading}

While the primary contribution of this paper is the geometric framework
for regime \emph{detection}, we briefly describe how the manifold
observables can be incorporated into a trading strategy for practical
validation.

\subsection{Signal Types and Weights}

Four signal types are extracted from the QCML geometry:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Curvature spike:} $|F_{ab}(t)|$ exceeds rolling threshold
  (weight $w_c=1.5$).
\item \textbf{Topology transition:} $|\Delta C_t|>\tau$
  (weight $w_\tau=2.0$).
\item \textbf{Metric expansion:} $\tr(g_{ab}(t))$ exceeds rolling
  threshold (weight $w_g=1.0$).
\item \textbf{Spectral warning:} $\Delta(t)$ drops below rolling
  threshold (weight $w_\Delta=1.0$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Position Sizing and Risk Management}

The aggregate signal strength
$s(t)=\sum_i w_i\,\mathbb{1}[\text{signal}_i(t)]$ determines position
sizing.  Positions are scaled inversely with signal magnitude:
$\text{position}(t) \propto -\text{sign}(s(t))\,|s(t)|$.

\subsection{Backtest Metrics}

Performance is evaluated using standard metrics: Sharpe ratio, Sortino
ratio, Calmar ratio (annualized return/max drawdown), maximum drawdown,
and win rate.  Transaction costs of 5\,bps per trade are included.

A full backtest evaluation of the topology-based trading strategy is
beyond the scope of this paper and is deferred to future work.  The
primary contribution here is the geometric detection framework itself;
trading applications would require additional considerations (execution,
slippage, portfolio construction) that merit separate treatment.

\section{Proofs of Formal Results}\label{sec:proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}]
Consider the eigenvalue equation $H(x)\ket{\psi(x)} = E_0(x)\ket{\psi(x)}$
subject to the normalization constraint $\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1$.  Define
the map $\Phi\colon\R^d\times\C^n\times\R\to\C^n\times\R$ by
\[
  \Phi(x,\psi,E) = \bigl(H(x)\psi - E\psi,\; \|\psi\|^2-1\bigr)\,.
\]
We have $\Phi(x_0,\psi_0,E_0)=0$ at any point where $\ket{\psi_0}$ is
the normalized ground state with eigenvalue $E_0$.  The Jacobian of
$\Phi$ with respect to $(\psi,E)$ at $(x_0,\psi_0,E_0)$ is
\[
  D_{(\psi,E)}\Phi = \begin{pmatrix}
    H(x_0)-E_0\,I & -\psi_0 \\
    2\psi_0^\dagger & 0
  \end{pmatrix}\,.
\]
The operator $H(x_0)-E_0\,I$ restricted to the orthogonal complement
$\psi_0^\perp$ is invertible with smallest singular value $\Delta(x_0)>0$
(the spectral gap).  The full Jacobian is therefore invertible whenever
$\Delta>0$.  By the implicit function theorem \cite{kato1966},
$(x\mapsto\psi(x), x\mapsto E_0(x))$ are $C^\infty$ functions of $x$ on
any open set where $\Delta>0$.

Since $H(x)$ depends polynomially on $x$ (it is a sum of squares of
affine functions), $\Phi$ is $C^\infty$ in all arguments.  The regularity
of $g_{ab}$ and $F_{ab}$ follows immediately from their definitions as
derivatives of $\ket{\psi(x)}$, and all higher geometric quantities
(Christoffel symbols, Ricci scalar, Chern numbers) are smooth as
compositions of smooth functions.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:chern}]
The ground state $\ket{\psi(x)}$ defines a $U(1)$ principal bundle (or
equivalently, a complex line bundle $\mathcal{L}$) over the parameter
surface $S$.  The Berry connection is the natural $U(1)$ connection on
$\mathcal{L}$:
\[
  \mathcal{A}_a(x) = i\braket{\psi(x)}{\partial_a\psi(x)}\,,
\]
and the Berry curvature $F_{ab} = \partial_a\mathcal{A}_b -
\partial_b\mathcal{A}_a$ is its curvature 2-form.

By the Chern--Weil theorem \cite{chern1946,nakahara2003}, the integral of
the curvature 2-form over any closed oriented surface equals $2\pi$ times
an integer:
\[
  \oint_S \frac{F}{2\pi} = c_1(\mathcal{L}) \in \mathbb{Z}\,,
\]
where $c_1(\mathcal{L})$ is the first Chern class of the line bundle
$\mathcal{L}\to S$.  This integer classifies the topological type of
$\mathcal{L}$: line bundles with different Chern numbers are
topologically inequivalent and cannot be smoothly deformed into each
other.

The invariance under smooth deformations follows from the fact that
$c_1\in H^2(S;\mathbb{Z})$ is a topological (cohomological) invariant:
any smooth family of Hamiltonians $H_s(x)$ that maintains $\Delta>0$
along $S$ defines a smooth family of line bundles with the same Chern
class.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:curvature_gap}]
Using first-order perturbation theory, the Berry connection can be
written as:
\[
  \mathcal{A}_a = i\braket{\psi_0}{\partial_a\psi_0}
  = i\sum_{n\neq 0} \frac{\dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_a H}{\psi_0}}{E_0-E_n}
  \braket{\psi_0}{\psi_n}\,,
\]
where $\ket{\psi_n}$ and $E_n$ are the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
$H(x)$.  Taking the curl:
\[
  F_{ab} = \partial_a\mathcal{A}_b - \partial_b\mathcal{A}_a
  = -2\,\mathrm{Im}\sum_{n\neq 0}
  \frac{\dbraket{\psi_0}{\partial_a H}{\psi_n}
        \dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_b H}{\psi_0}}{(E_n-E_0)^2}\,.
\]
Taking absolute values and using the triangle inequality:
\[
  |F_{ab}| \leq 2\sum_{n\neq 0}
  \frac{|\dbraket{\psi_0}{\partial_a H}{\psi_n}|\,
        |\dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_b H}{\psi_0}|}{(E_n-E_0)^2}\,.
\]
Since $E_n-E_0\geq\Delta$ for all $n\neq 0$:
\[
  |F_{ab}| \leq \frac{1}{\Delta^2}\;
  2\sum_{n\neq 0}
  |\dbraket{\psi_0}{\partial_a H}{\psi_n}|\,
  |\dbraket{\psi_n}{\partial_b H}{\psi_0}|
  = \frac{K}{\Delta^2}\,,
\]
where $K$ depends only on the matrix elements of $\partial_a H$ and
$\partial_b H$ between eigenstates, not on the gap.  This completes
the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:qfi_volume}]
Let $g_{ab}$ have eigendecomposition $g = V\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,
\ldots,\lambda_d)\,V^T$ with $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_r > 0 =
\lambda_{r+1}=\cdots=\lambda_d$.  The Riemannian volume element on the
non-degenerate $r$-dimensional subspace is
$\mathrm{vol}_r = \sqrt{\det(g|_r)}$, where $g|_r$ is the restriction
of $g$ to the span of the first $r$ eigenvectors.  Then
\[
  \det(g|_r) = \prod_{i=1}^r \lambda_i = \det^+(g)\,.
\]
Thus $\det^+(g) = (\mathrm{vol}_r)^2$.

The interpretation follows directly:
\begin{enumerate}
\item When $\det^+(g)\to 0$, either the effective rank $r$ decreases
  (eigenvalues crossing zero) or some eigenvalues approach zero,
  indicating dimensional collapse of the quantum state space---the
  market state is confined to a lower-dimensional subspace,
  characteristic of phase boundaries where correlations increase.
\item Large $\det^+(g)$ indicates that the quantum metric has full rank
  with large eigenvalues: the market state is sensitive to perturbations
  in all directions, characteristic of a stable, multi-factor regime
  where diverse risk factors are active.  \qedhere
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Learned Operators}\label{app:learned_ops}

The PCA-based operator construction is a stated limitation of our
framework.  We explore end-to-end differentiable operator learning via
parameterized Hermitian matrices
$A_k = V_k\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_k)\,V_k^\dagger$, trained to
maximize a differentiable proxy for Cohen's $d$.

In leave-one-crisis-out evaluation, the learned operators fail to match
PCA-based operators: mean $d_{\text{learned}} = 0.000$ vs.\
$d_{\text{PCA}} = 0.927$ (paired $t$-test: $t = -3.995$, $p = 0.002$).
The learned operators collapse to trivial solutions across all 12
held-out crises, despite convergence on training data.  This negative
result is informative: maximizing Cohen's $d$ directly is ill-conditioned
because (i) the differentiable $d$ proxy has vanishing gradients when
crisis/non-crisis score distributions overlap, and (ii) the Cayley map
parameterization does not constrain the spectral properties that make
PCA operators physically meaningful.  This validates the continued use
of PCA-based operators while identifying operator learning as the
primary avenue for future improvement.

\subsection{SDE Empirical Validation}\label{app:sde_valid}

We compare simulated paths from the geometric SDE (metric-induced
diffusion) and a standard SDE (isotropic diffusion, same drift) to
actual market returns using K-S tests and Anderson-Darling tests.

The geometric SDE produces simulated return distributions closer to
reality: mean K-S statistic of $0.274$ vs.\ $0.321$ for the standard
SDE, outperforming in 4 of 5 PCA dimensions.  The geometric SDE also
generates significantly more regime-like transitions: mean metric
eigenvalue spikes per path is $63.7$ (geometric) vs.\ $22.3$ (standard),
with $p < 10^{-6}$ (Mann--Whitney).  This confirms that the metric
tensor captures genuine dynamical structure beyond what isotropic
diffusion can reproduce.
